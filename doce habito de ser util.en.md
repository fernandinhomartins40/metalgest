The Sweet Habit of Being Useful

Prologue: The Mirror and the Mask

Helena stared at her own reflection in the narrow bathroom mirror of the Tourism Office as if she were facing a hostile witness. The white ceiling light spared nothing, except precisely what she wished it would reveal: her age. At thirty-eight, she still had the same face with smooth cheeks, a clear forehead, and large eyes that had often made her hear, more times than she could count, phrases like 'you look like a girl,' 'this little face is deceiving,' and 'once you grow a little, you'll go far.' The problem wasn't looking young. The problem was that, in that city, youth in a woman almost always meant permission to underestimate her.

She opened her purse, chose the darkest lipstick, and applied the color with the precision of someone performing an ancient rite. She tried to add, to her mouth, the weight that the world refused to recognize in her voice. The cell phone vibrated on the marble countertop.

Hey, remember to call the car insurance. The broker asked for the policyholder's CPF and I couldn't find it. Kiss.

Ricardo.

Helena read the message once. Then again. There was no explicit cruelty, and maybe that was exactly what was most exhausting. It wasn't an insult. It wasn't a shout. It was the kind of request that only exists when someone is sure it will be fulfilled. Ricardo didn't ask if she could handle it. Ricardo lived in the certainty that she was already handling it.

She put the cell phone away without answering and exhaled slowly. On the sink, someone had left two cups with coffee remnants and a crumpled piece of sugar paper. A small domestic disaster transplanted to the city hall. Helena picked up the paper reflexively, threw it in the trash, and immediately felt angry at herself. Even alone, without witnesses, she still corrected other people's mess.

Outside, the hallway was boiling. The city hall building had the old smell of public offices: reheated coffee, cheap wax, archive mold, and tired air conditioning. Helena knew those smells like someone knows the rooms of their own house. She had been moving around there for ten years with folders, spreadsheets, technical notes, and crises extinguished before they reached the men who later appeared smiling in the official photograph. If the city worked, there was a portion of that functioning that passed silently through her hands.

She adjusted the gray blazer, checked if the collar was straight, and left the bathroom with the old posture of someone who had become an expert in taking up little space without losing efficiency. The day hadn't even started yet, and Helena already felt the weight of an invisible piano resting on her shoulders.

Part I: The Fall of the Invisible Helena

Chapter 1: The Ghost Advisor

The third-floor hallway was noisier than usual. The mayor would hold a press conference at the end of the morning to present the new waterfront project, and whenever the name 'investor' circulated around the city hall, the men in suits would start walking a little faster, as if the hurry itself were a sign of competence.

Helena crossed the corridor carrying a worn leather folder, a notebook, and a thermal coffee cup that she still hadn't managed to drink. Before reaching her own office, a hand appeared through the half-open door of the Communications Office and pulled her inside.

— You are the embodiment of divine mercy — said Caio, without giving her time to react.

He was in front of two monitors, with three windows open at the same time and his hair disheveled from someone who had spent the morning putting out political fires with a bad Wi-Fi password and a lot of irony. At thirty-five, Caio was the smartest person in the Communications Office and, for that very reason, would never be treated as such in public. For the top brass, he was "the social media kid," useful for making pretty videos and turning disaster into palatable captions.

— "If this is another presentation that was promised without consulting the technical team, I want to charge for emotional overtime," said Helena.

Caio held out his hands like someone surrendering.

— Dr. Alberto wants the final numbers of the waterfront consortium. Marcos Aurélio sent a version of the presentation in which the word 'sustainability' appears six times, but the erosion data disappeared. The man turned geology into an advertising piece.

Helena set the briefcase on his desk and opened the laptop.

— Because he doesn't understand the data. And because he thinks that nobody in the audience understands it either.

— The problem is that today there will be real journalists at the press conference. Not just bloggers fed with coffee break.

She located the right file in less than twenty seconds. She adjusted the risk projection chart, repositioned a budget impact table, and quickly reviewed the mayor's speech, swapping two clichés for phrases that seemed more solid.

Caio watched in silence, with that old mixture of admiration and sadness.

— Sometimes I find it offensive how much you function — he said.

Helena raised an eyebrow.

— Was that a compliment?

— It was a report. You save this building more than the fire brigade, and yet you remain invisible.

She closed the notebook and handed him the file on the USB drive.

— Not invisible. Discreet.

— Helena, you are not discreet. You were made discreet.

That made her laugh, despite the tiredness. It was one of Caio's talents: delivering truths with a sparkle of sarcasm that prevented the person from defending themselves too quickly.

— The mayor called me at half past nine — she said, picking up the thermal cup again. — Maybe he will finally formalize my appointment.

Caio rested his hip on the table and crossed his arms.

— Appointment for what?

— Special advisor.

He let out a short laugh, without humor.

— Sure. They will finally promote the woman who does the work. What a revolutionary novelty.

— Caio.

— I am trying to temper my hope so as not to seem like a child on Christmas Eve.

Helena pretended not to be bothered. She had been hearing vague promises from Alberto for months: "in the next renovation," "after the waterfront project," "when this funding is finalized." Every time she delivered another solution, the recognition seemed to move a little further ahead, like a line painted on the asphalt.

At nine twenty-seven, the mayor's chief of staff appeared at the door.

— Helena, Dr. Alberto asked you to go upstairs now.

The mayor's office occupied the best part of the building: large windows, a view of the main square, dark wood, overly large armchairs, and the expensive silence of someone who never answers their own door. Alberto was standing next to the desk, wearing that overly white smile that worked well in a campaign and poorly up close.

— Helena, my technical savior — he said, opening his arms in a welcoming gesture that never reached his eyes. — Sit, my daughter.

My daughter.

She sat down without correcting. She had been swallowing paternalistic vocatives for years as one swallows an unavoidable medicine.

— I saw the material you finished. Brilliant, as always. You have a rare talent for organizing chaos without making noise.

Helena maintained her posture.

— Thank you.

— And that's precisely why I need you so much where you are.

She knew that curve of the conversation. Even so, the body reacted first. The fingers stiffened on the knee, the shoulder blades closed.

— I understand.

Alberto walked slowly to the window, as if searching for the right words in the reflection of the square.

— I decided to appoint Marcos Aurélio to the Special Advisory for coordination with investors.

Helena heard the sentence as if someone had repeated it underwater.

— Marcos? — he asked, before he could avoid it.

— Look. You are technically brilliant. But Marcos has... political clout. White hair. Connections. You know how Brasília is. Sometimes, at a tough table, image matters as much as the resume.

The image.

It was always the image. His apparent youth as a flaw, his competence as someone else's asset.

— I am thirty-eight years old, mayor — she said, with enough delicacy not to sound defiant and enough firmness not to sound defeated.

— Sure, sure. But you know how people see.

She could have remembered that it was she who secured the semester's budget amendments, she who reorganized the waterfront project, she who had stayed up three nights the previous year to prevent the city hall from losing a federal grant due to an error by the Treasury. She could have said that Marcos Aurélio confused bidding with favor and that he needed help to open a spreadsheet. She could have said a lot of things.

He/She just said:

— I understand.

Alberto approached and tapped his shoulder twice lightly, the most effective gesture he knew to reduce an adult to a place of subordination.

— You are indispensable, Helena. What would I do without you to clean up other people's mess?

She knew, at that moment, that she had received the most precise definition of her own imprisonment.

Essential.

The word, when it came from a man in power, rarely meant recognition. It meant usefulness without promotion. It meant: I can't lose you in the machinery because no one wants to do the dirty work with your competence.

As she left the office, the hallway seemed colder. Caio was waiting for her leaning against the water cooler, reading her face even before she opened her mouth.

— Did they name the fossil? — he asked.

Helena nodded.

Caio closed his eyes for a second.

— I hate always being right about things that hurt you.

She tried to smile, but she couldn't. The cellphone vibrated again.

I forgot to tell you: my father is coming to dinner tomorrow. Make that eggplant lasagna. Yours is the only one he eats.

Helena kept looking at the screen a little longer than necessary.

On the other side of the hallway, the mayor's office door opened. Marcos Aurélio entered laughing loudly, chest puffed out, light-colored tie, the loose authority of someone who never had to deserve the position he holds. Alberto hugged him with the theatrical enthusiasm reserved for men who help compose a photograph.

Helena put away the cell phone.

At that moment, for the first time in many years, the habit of being useful did not seem like a virtue to him. It seemed like a vice.

Chapter 2: Car Insurance

Ricardo liked to say that he was a simple man. The phrase always came out with calm pride, as if simplicity were a moral quality and not the elegant name of a comfort managed by someone else. In practice, Ricardo's simplicity depended on an invisible architecture: clean towels in the cupboard, bills paid, tires inflated, medicines restocked, bank password written down somewhere, gift for his mother-in-law bought in advance, glass of water by the bed, dinner in the oven when he got home from work. Almost all of that had Helena's hands on it.

The morning after the conversation with Alberto, she woke up at five forty-two, two minutes before the alarm. The habit of waking up before things was both a talent and a disease. She lay there for a few seconds, staring at the ceiling, while her mind organized the day's list at automatic speed: separate light and dark clothes, check the expiration date of her mother's health plan, review the press conference material, respond to the bank email about the final renegotiation of the last 2018 debt, call Ricardo's car insurance.

Next to her, Ricardo slept on his side, breathing deeply, an arm flung over the pillow where Helena rarely managed to rest completely. He was forty-one years old, with a neatly trimmed beard, the kind of beauty that aged in a man's favor. When awake, he occupied spaces with the confidence of someone who never doubted belonging to them.

Helena got up without making a sound, tied her hair, and went to the kitchen. She made the coffee, turned on the machine, separated the morning fruit, heated the bread, picked up the shirt she had ironed the night before, and hung it on the chair in the living room. When Ricardo appeared, already dressed in dress pants and with his cell phone in hand, the apartment seemed to have woken up alone.

— Good morning, Hê," he said, approaching the bench. "Did you call the insurance?"

She opened the fridge to get the cheese.

— Not yet. I will call during the break of the press conference.

— If you can do this before lunch, it helps. The broker is slow.

Help.

Helena served the coffee in the two cups.

— Today is the day of the boardwalk press conference.

Ricardo sat down.

— Ah, really. Will there be a lot of press?

— Go.

— So make it great. You know these events matter.

She stared at him for a second.

— I know.

Ricardo bit a piece of bread and pointed to the shirt spread out on the chair.

— Thanks for setting it aside. Yesterday I got home exhausted.

Helena leaned on the counter, without sitting down.

— I talked to the mayor yesterday.

— What's up?

— He appointed Marcos Aurélio.

Ricardo made a small "hm" through his nose, the exact sound of someone who received unpleasant information, but not life-changing.

— Wow. Bummer.

— Boring?

He lifted his eyes from the plate.

— No. It's unfair. I'm saying it's annoying in the sense of bad.

Helena pulled up the chair and sat down facing him.

— I organized the entire project. Marcos can't read half of the reports.

— I know you do a lot, Hê. I'm just saying that high positions are also a lot about image. Marcos has more of the profile.

She almost laughed. Almost.

— Profile.

— You know how these men think. Maybe Alberto is protecting you.

Helena felt something close inside her with the precision of a hinge.

— Protecting myself from what?

He raised one of his hands, conciliatory.

— Don't start. I'm on your side. I just think that sometimes you want to prove too much. You already earn well, have stability, internal traffic. Not everyone was born to be in the picture.

There it was. It wasn't malice. It was order. The old, the comfortable, the one that fit in every house and office in the city: men appear, women hold up.

Helena took a sip of coffee that was already cold.

— What time is your father coming tomorrow?

Ricardo relaxed, as if the conversation had returned to safe tracks.

— Half past eight. If you can, also leave that zucchini appetizer. He mentioned it once and went crazy.

She watched him chewing. He was a polite man, pleasant in public, incapable of recognizing himself as the center of a structure. Ricardo's brutality did not come from the outburst; it came from the natural way he expected to be served.

After he left, the apartment plunged into the clean silence of organized homes. Helena picked up his cup, washed the knife, closed the cabinet, checked if the stove was off. Then, she opened the laundry cabinet and found herself standing in front of the bottles labeled by color, function, and date.

In that kitchen, there were more systems than in half of the city hall offices.

And none of them had his name.

On the way to work, driving through the lukewarm morning traffic, Helena remembered the first time someone had praised her for 'handling everything.' She was eight years old. Her mother had returned tired from sewing and found the table set, the rice warmed, and her younger brother already bathed. Celina had held her daughter's face with both hands and said, with eyes wet with relief:

— My girl is a relief.

Helena had spent her life trying to repeat the feeling of that sentence.

Being rest.

To be the person who prevents the house from collapsing.

Be so necessary that no one dares to leave her.

The problem is that, over time, the needs of others become an address where we disappear.

When she arrived at the city hall, she found the building in a state of makeup. Plants hurriedly watered, crystal glasses being arranged in the Main Hall, men in suits adjusting their ties in front of the reflection of the glass doors. Helena went through all of that in silence, already certain that, from then on, every little request made to her would sound different.

Not because they had changed.

Because, finally, she had begun to listen.

Interlude I: The Girl Who Anticipated Everything

Before Helena learned to call it a habit, the behavior already existed in the body like someone who exists in a family before becoming language. The house where she grew up had two bedrooms, a narrow hallway, and a kitchen that always seemed a little smaller than the day demanded. Celina sewed outward. Jorge, her father, went through life like many honest men insufficiently trained in delicacy: he worked a lot, spoke little, believed that being present was more than enough.

Helena was eight years old when she discovered that anticipation brought peace. Her mother arrived one night, almost eleven o'clock, from sewing, with stiff shoulders and eyes blurred from thread and fabric. She found the pot covered, the rice reheated on low heat, her younger brother already bathed, and the living room floor swept. The girl had done everything in order, misjudging the doneness of the beans, using too much detergent, and forgetting to close the bathroom window, but enough for her mother to lean on the sink and cry with relief.

— My girl is a relief — Celina said, holding her daughter's face with both hands.

It wasn't a cruel phrase. It was worse. It was a loving phrase full of need. Helena learned to seek that specific sparkle in her mother's eyes, the emotion of someone who feels spared by someone smaller and, precisely because of that, extraordinary. Every time she managed to produce it, she experienced an irresistible mixture of importance and belonging.

At school, the logic just changed scenery. The teacher liked her because she turned in everything early, collected her classmates' papers, explained exercises without mocking anyone, remembered the principal's birthday, and helped organize the June festival. "Helena is a good girl," they said. "Helena doesn't cause trouble." "Helena was born ready." When some boy kicked a chair or answered back rudely, she felt both strangeness and envy at the same time. Strangeness because the disorder seemed risky to her. Envy because there seemed to be, in that rough courage, a territory she would never be allowed to enter.

Once, at twelve years old, he forgot to heat the beans because he was studying for a science test. Jorge arrived tired, opened the cold pot, and asked, without raising his voice:

— Did something happen?

That was all. No yelling. No real scolding. But Helena kept the sentence as a moral failing. Something had happened: she had thought of herself first for two hours. After that, she started studying with one ear on the pot.

In adolescence, she became pretty too early for many people's taste and serious too early for almost everyone's taste. Her mother kept saying that an intelligent girl didn't need to attract attention. Her aunt said that a good man looks for a woman who 'holds things together.' A neighbor taught her how to fold a fitted sheet as if passing on a civilizational inheritance. No one explicitly told her that her worth would depend on her ability to cushion the world. She didn't need it. The city's female upbringing did that work by osmosis.

When he won a scholarship in a technical course and started coming home later, he learned another layer of the same mechanism. If he helped his brother with the lesson before leaving, if he left the salad washed, if he prepared the lunch for the next day in advance, the absence would be forgiven. Freedom, he understood without formulating, was something that needed to be compensated.

In the last year of high school, a Portuguese teacher corrected one of her essays with blue ink and wrote at the top: "You think like someone who organizes a house on fire." Helena took the sentence home as a compliment. Only many years later would she realize how much diagnosis was in it.

At eighteen, when she said she wanted to work in production and events, Celina sat at the table with the expression of someone assessing a dangerous crossing.

— Daughter, just be careful not to become one of those women who do everything on their own and then no one can stand it.

Helena smiled, promised caution, served dinner, and cleared the dishes. She hadn't understood the entire sentence, but she kept the warning in a corner. Becoming unbearably autonomous was, for the women she loved, a risk almost as fearsome as depending too much on someone.

Years later, already an adult, she would still fall into the same trap: doing everything alone so as not to bother anyone and, in the process, building around herself a world in which everyone thought it was normal to never ask if she needed help.

Chapter 3: The Lasagna of Discord

Helena's father-in-law had the habit of arriving ten minutes before the appointed time and acting as if he were being received in a restaurant whose service should already be fully operational. Ricardo's father, Mr. Oswaldo, had been a widower for six years, wore strong cologne, and an old-fashioned courtesy that, being so rehearsed, served more to keep distance than to welcome.

On the night of the dinner, Helena left the city hall after eight, her head heavy from the press conference and the bitter taste of humiliation still lingering on the roof of her mouth. The event had ended worse than Alberto had imagined and better than she had dared to predict. Marcos Aurélio had stammered in front of a simple question about coastal erosion; Alberto had called her to the stage to 'clarify the matter'; Helena had said, in a clear voice, that even the most optimistic scenario was bad and that the report had been delivered in advance. That was enough. Nothing heroic. Nothing theatrical. Just the truth without cushioning. Even so, the entire room understood what was happening: the man officially responsible for the project had no idea what he had signed.

Alberto whispered her name in the hallway afterward, promising a "very serious" talk the next day. Helena pretended not to hear. She had stopped by the market on the way home because the lasagna cheese had run out in the morning and because, after all, she continued following the old tracks.

When he entered the apartment, the table was already set. Not by Ricardo. By Helena from a few hours earlier, who had left everything partially ready before leaving. Sliced eggplants in the colander, sauce prepared in the fridge, cloth napkins, separated glasses, savory pie in the low oven. It was like walking into a trap set by his own hand.

From the living room, Oswaldo's voice arrived before his body.

— The daughter-in-law has arrived.

Ricardo appeared in the hallway, loose tie, tense smile.

— I thought you were going to take longer.

— I took longer.

He tried to kiss her and she offered her cheek with distraction.

— Your father is already here.

— I saw.

Oswaldo got up from the armchair and opened his arms.

— My kitchen mayor.

Helena smiled just enough not to seem rude.

— Good evening, Oswaldo.

— This city should be managed with your efficiency, you know? — he said, sitting down again. — An organized woman makes a difference anywhere. But nowadays people have forgotten the value of behind-the-scenes work.

Ricardo laughed.

— Say that to her, dad. Helena has been wanting to save the world.

She set the bag on the chair and went to the kitchen. She needed a few seconds away from the two of them. She turned on the oven, took out the sauce, arranged the slices of eggplant on a baking tray. Her hands knew exactly what to do, and that might have been the worst part. Even tired, even wounded, even with a deep anger scratching at her chest, she remained functional.

Ricardo appeared at the door, holding a beer.

— You've been acting strange since yesterday.

Helena spread a thin layer of sauce.

— The mayor gave the position to Marcos.

— I know. You already spoke.

— And today he tried to use me to fix his embarrassment on open network.

— But did you fix it?

She stopped with the spoon in the air.

— No.

Ricardo frowned.

— What do you mean?

— I did not lie.

It took him a second to understand.

— Helena.

The metallic dish reflected the yellow light of the kitchen. The sauce slowly dripped between the layers.

— The project is bad, Ricardo.

— That doesn't matter in the middle of a press conference.

— That always matters.

— Don't be naive.

The word fell between the two with a quiet weight.

— Naive?

— You know what I'm talking about. Politics is not a thesis. Sometimes you hold the information, resolve it internally, and protect the team.

— The team?

He drank a sip of beer.

— Alberto, Marcos, management.

Helena placed both hands on the counter and stared at him.

— And who protects me, Ricardo?

He seemed sincerely confused.

— I protect you.

It was there that something in Helena went off balance.

Not because of the sentence. Because of the serenity with which it was said. Ricardo truly believed he was protecting her. She had been feeding on this narrative for years: during the financial collapse of 2018, when he had paid three months of rent; on the nights he had advised her to 'take it easy'; every time he had recommended less ambition, more caution, less friction, more discretion. The name he gave to this was protection. The name she was beginning to see was restraint.

From the living room, Oswaldo called:

— Son, open the wine. Helena must be hungry.

Ricardo lowered his voice.

— Make the lasagna, serve dinner, and tomorrow you talk to Alberto. Don't start a fight you don't need to start.

Helena looked at the empty form in front of her. She thought about the last few years reduced to the logic of that kitchen: anticipate, serve, correct, cushion. She thought about the mayor, about Marcos, about her father-in-law, about her husband, about the whole city resting on tired women who heard compliments as payment. She thought about her own mother calling her a relief. She thought about how many times she had confused love with need.

Then he/she pushed the mold away.

— I am not going to make the lasagna.

Ricardo laughed, because the sentence seemed impossible to him.

— Okay.

— No. I am not going to do it.

The following silence was so concrete that it seemed to have weight.

— My father is in the living room.

— I know.

— Helena, this is not the time for theater.

— It’s also not the time for another night when I do everything and you pretend it comes out ready.

Ricardo dropped the bottle on the counter with force.

— You are deducting the city hall from me.

— No. I am finally putting the two things together.

He ran his hand over his face, irritated.

— You mean you're going to leave my father without dinner because you felt undervalued at work?

Helena felt a strange calm settle in the center of her body. It was not serenity. It was rupture.

— Your father won't go without dinner. There is pie, bread, cheese, salad, and an adult man with both hands working in the living room.

— Are you crazy?

— No. I think I started.

She took the apron from the hook without putting it on, folded it, and put it in the drawer. Then, she went to the living room.

Oswaldo raised his eyes, waiting for the announcement of dinner.

— The lasagna won't come out today, Oswaldo," said Helena. "If you want, I can heat the pie."

The old man blinked, bewildered.

— What do you mean you're not going out?

— Exactly like that.

Ricardo appeared behind her.

— Heh.

But she no longer wanted to cushion anyone's discomfort.

— I had a bad day, I'm exhausted, and I'm not going to pretend everything is fine to keep the evening pleasant for the men of the house.

Oswaldo adjusted himself in the armchair, offended.

— I am not understanding this tone.

— Maybe because you never need to understand the work it takes for a night like this to happen.

No one answered immediately. The television, turned on at a low volume on the shelf, seemed to come from another apartment.

Helena turned around, went into the room, and locked the door. Outside, she heard Ricardo say something in a nervous whisper, then her father's indignant voice, then chairs being dragged.

She did not cry.

He opened the notebook on the small desk by the window and clicked on a nameless folder he had kept for months, hidden among work documents. Inside it was a file called "Structural Projects 2027-2030." He opened it. The pages appeared on the screen with an almost aggressive clarity: mobility, drainage, daycare centers, community-based tourism, transparency plan, waterfront restoration, lean budget, goals, sources of funding.

That was there even before she admitted that she wanted something bigger.

On the cell phone, a message from Caio appeared:

Still serving the patriarchy or already at the stage of domestic rebellion?

Helena read and, for the first time in days, truly smiled.

"Advanced stage," he/she answered.

Chapter 4: The Year Everything Fell Apart

In the early hours of that same night, while Ricardo slept on the sofa by his own dramatic choice and the apartment held the smell of the dinner that hadn't happened as promised, Helena sat on the bedroom floor with the laptop closed on her lap. She couldn't sleep because the body, when it finally receives permission to say no, takes time to learn that the house will not collapse in that very moment.

It was then that 2018 came back with the intact force of poorly buried memories.

At the time, Helena was thirty years old, ran a small event production company called Brisa Alta, and had the uncomfortable conviction that competence was enough. She worked a lot, charged little, knew suppliers by name, set up stages, wrote projects, negotiated with sponsors, and even wiped the floor in her own office when the cleaning lady was absent. She enjoyed the thrill of creating something from scratch and seeing the city fit into a schedule.

A Brisa Alta was not large, but it had a reputation. It had organized regional fairs, health congresses, gastronomic festivals, store openings, two business meetings, and a season of small seaside shows. Helena dreamed of expanding. That was when the contract appeared, which seemed like the ultimate breakthrough: the organization of a major coastal tourism forum financed by a consortium of companies with informal support from the city hall.

Ricardo, at the time boyfriend for just over a year, celebrated as if the victory were theirs.

— Here we go — he said, opening a bottle of sparkling wine in her apartment. — This contract changes your life.

It changed. Not in the way he imagined.

The consortium delayed the first installment. Then the second. Helena used working capital, renegotiated with the supplier, sold the camera she used for smaller jobs, postponed changing the car's tires, slept four hours a night. When she pushed for payment, she heard the first indecent suggestion.

The financial intermediary, a man named Varella, received her in a room with polar air conditioning and a photo of the mayor in a discreet picture frame.

— The process is stuck," he said, tapping lightly on a briefcase. "But there is a faster way, if you agree to adjust the grades.

— Adapt how?

— Slightly inflate the rental service and redistribute two items. Small stuff. Everyone does it.

Helena remembered, even today, the immediate nausea.

— No.

Varella opened a patient smile, as if speaking to a stubborn child.

— So you are going to wait.

She waited. And, while she waited, she paid the team, the infrastructure, the setup, the transportation, the food, the accommodation. The event happened. It was praised. Beautiful photos appeared in the local newspapers. The consortium disappeared behind invented bureaucracies. One supplier protested a title. Another charged legally. In three months, her name was in the registry, in the bank, on the tip of the tongue of people who had never known how hard she worked.

On the afternoon the bailiff rang the doorbell, it was raining. Helena opened the door wearing an old sweatshirt and a hair clip. The man was polite, almost embarrassed. He had large hands and a brown envelope.

— Mrs. Helena Duarte?

She signed the receipt with cold fingers. The company was going into foreclosure due to accumulated debt. There were too many numbers on those pages, and they all said the same thing: the floor had been removed.

Ricardo arrived later and found her sitting at the kitchen table, the papers spread out in front of her.

— I warned you it was too big for you to handle on your own — I said, in a tone meant to be practical, not cruel.

At the time, the phrase hurt like a sentence. Years later, Helena would understand that this had been the first brick of the domestic prison that he would help her build.

She sold tables, lost the commercial space, dissolved a partnership, fired two people while crying, was called a fraud by a man who owed her more than she owed to the world. She paid what she could. She financed the rest. She accepted a temporary technical position at the Tourism Department because the city hall needed someone who understood production, fundraising, and execution. Alberto received her with the paternal smile of someone who considers himself a benefactor.

— You are too competent to be out of the market — he had said. — Here there will be stability. And protection.

The word again.

Protection.

At that time, Helena was too tired to notice the hidden cost. She came in, worked, delivered far more than was asked, and ended up staying. Ricardo began paying some rents, helping her with installments, and publicly repeating that the two of them had saved each other. When they got married, three years later, the story was already established: Helena was brilliant, but needed grounding; Ricardo was thoughtful, stable, the man who had helped her get her life on track.

What no one saw was that all the tracks were heading in the same direction: less risk, less voice, less declared ambition.

In the darkness of the room, Helena placed her hand on the last renegotiation file she still kept. It was almost time to finally settle the liability from that disaster. For years, 2018 had been the definitive argument to make her back down. Each fear received the same silent push: remember what happens when you want too much.

But there was a truth buried beneath the shame: she hadn't broken out of incompetence. She broke because she had refused a fraud in a game designed to reward exactly the opposite of what she valued.

At three nineteen in the morning, Helena opened the laptop again and wrote, at the top of the project document:

"Those who know the depths can recognize the lie on the surface."

Slept little. Woke up determined.

Interlude II: The First Man Who Took the Credit

Helena's first job was at a small corporate event production company called Horizonte Sul, located in a stuffy commercial room on the third floor of a building without an elevator. The owner, Álvaro, had the vague age of men who cultivate youthfulness as a strategy of control and wore a dress shirt with rolled-up sleeves to seem hands-on. He was the one who told her this in the interview:

— I like organized women. Men who are too creative give me a headache.

At the time, Helena found the sentence flattering.

She started as an administrative assistant and, in less than four months, she was already handling suppliers, assembly schedules, accreditation, badge final artwork, coffee breaks, cost spreadsheets, and last-minute crises. Álvaro was the type who treated female competence as a personal discovery. He introduced her to clients with sentences like 'this girl is my right hand' and 'the brain of the operation is back here.' He laughed when saying it, as if partial recognition were generous enough.

At the first major event she helped organize, a health conference for five hundred people, Helena spent three nights redoing the schedule because the sales manager had sold the client a structure that was impossible for the agreed price. She slept on an inflatable mattress in the company's office, took quick showers in the floor's bathroom, and ate cream cracker biscuits with lukewarm coffee while aligning the cost spreadsheet.

On the opening morning, the event got started on time, with no sound failure, no supplier freaking out, no speaker lost. The city's health secretary praised the organization in front of everyone.

Álvaro responded with a broad gesture to the team.

— Here we wear the shirt.

Later, already at the closing, a client approached Helena backstage.

— Congratulations. They told me it was you who held this here.

She didn't have time to thank him. Álvaro appeared from behind, hand on her shoulder, ready smile.

— Helena is great in support - he said. "But I'm the one who closes the drawing.

It was a simple sentence, almost administrative. Even so, Helena felt something shift inside her. Not because she imagined she deserved full public glory. But because she perceived, confusingly, that the word support functioned like a revolving door: it let in work, made credit go out.

The following year, the company received a proposal to organize the launch of a luxury condominium. Álvaro asked her to set up the entire flow, reception, and customer experience strategy. Helena designed the event, recommended suppliers, created the presentation script for the real estate agents, and even suggested the lighting for the central model. At the final meeting with the construction company, she sat next to him with her laptop open.

When he started to explain the plan, Álvaro interrupted:

— Let me translate for the director, okay?

She fell silent. He repeated everything with less precise words and much greater confidence. In the end, the director shook his hand, praised 'his vision,' and requested two more changes. Álvaro agreed without consulting her. Later, in the elevator, he lightly tapped her arm.

— You need to understand one thing, Helena. The client trusts the leader. Too much technique scares.

That day, she came home wanting to quit her job and with a nameless guilt for wanting that. It still took three more years for her to leave. During that time, she learned to do twice the work to prove half the worth, and to smile when they heard her idea from a man's mouth and called it brilliant.

When, years later, Alberto would say that she was indispensable and Ricardo would call her a pillar, Helena's body would already recognize the trick. Not enough to escape immediately. But enough for each compliment to be accompanied by a light itch, almost imperceptible, like synthetic fabric in the heat.

Chapter 5: The Refuge of the Invisible

The bar was called Refuge and was located in a basement in the old downtown, behind a closed record store and next to a building that always seemed about to collapse. Helena would never have gone there alone. Not because the place was dangerous, but because, for years, she had learned to frequent only places where her presence was easy to explain.

Caio was waiting for her at the counter, holding a blue glass that probably contained alcohol, sugar, and a fair amount of irony.

— You came with the face of a woman who has already committed a crime and hasn't told the police yet — he said, when she approached.

Helena sat down.

— I didn't make the lasagna.

Caio brought his hand to his chest.

— So there was, in fact, institutional breakdown in the Brazilian home.

She laughed. The sound came out tired, but real.

The Refuge was full without feeling cramped. There were small tables, dark walls, old movie posters, and a DJ playing songs Helena didn’t know, although she recognized in the beat a sort of permission. Inside, no one behaved as if they were accountable to an invisible authority. Couples kissed without looking around, groups laughed loudly, men wore glitter on their faces, women took the center of conversations. Caio seemed more whole in that environment than in any hallway of the city hall.

— You seem different here — said Helena.

— I stay whole here.

The answer landed between the two with the naturalness of something already known and never said.

Caio spun the glass by the straw.

— Alberto lost control of the narrative today.

— Not yet.

— Have you noticed how people in power always seem invincible until someone stops plugging the leak from below?

Helena asked for sparkling water. She didn't want any kind of mist. She needed to feel every piece moving.

— He is going to call me tomorrow.

— He will straighten you out, offer you an honorable way out, or pretend it was all a technical misunderstanding. The complete kit.

— You speak as if you knew the script.

— I have been writing half of it for four years.

She watched him. There was tiredness in Caio's eyes, but also something sharper. He was less willing to be funny than usual.

— What do you want to tell me, Caio?

He rested his forearms on the counter and lowered his voice.

— That you have a choice to make. Continue being the woman who saves mediocre men behind the scenes or accept that, if this city has any chance of changing its face, it will need someone who knows the machine from the inside and does not belong to it by birth.

Helena almost responded with caution. The reflex came quickly: don't travel, this is bigger than me, politics is dirty, I am a technician, I don't have the profile. Instead, she asked:

— Are you serious?

— More than in any meme I have ever published with public funds.

Caio pulled a black notebook from his coat pocket and placed it in front of her.

— Start by writing down everything.

— Everything what?

— Everything you have ever done and never got credit for. Everything you corrected. Everything you saw. Everything you heard because no one realized that the girl with the young face was paying attention.

Helena ran her fingers over the cover.

— A notebook of grudges?

— A notebook of evidence.

She remained silent.

On the dance floor, two women danced without enough music to justify the joy. A waiter passed by laughing with a tall tray of glasses. Caio watched her as one who waits for a decision he knows he cannot make for her.

— The Renewal Party is starving — he said. — No strong name, no money, no access. Alberto co-opted almost everyone who knew how to run a campaign. What do they have? Tired people. Invisible people. People who work and don't show up. You know this electorate because you are this electorate.

— Caio, I have no background, no money, no last name, I have nothing...

— You have credibility where it matters.

— Where does it matter to whom?

He smiled sideways.

— For those who clean, serve, carry, tidy up, fix things, and go home without a photo. For those who work themselves to exhaustion and hear that they should be grateful for being needed. For those who see a secretary make a mistake and think: I would do better. Do you know how many women will understand the phrase "I didn't make the lasagna" without you needing to explain a single line?

That hit her with an uncomfortable clarity. The lasagna wasn't about eggplant, the oven, or dinner. It was about the whole mechanism.

— And Ricardo? — he asked, more quietly.

Caio didn't even need to think.

— Ricardo loves the version of you that keeps his world soft. The other one, the one that wants to exist outside of work, he will call uncontrolled.

Helena looked around. The Refuge was a basement full of people who, outside, would be advised to lower their voices. In there, no one lowered anything.

He/She picked up the notebook.

— I don't know if I want to be a candidate.

— I didn't ask you for candidacy today. I asked for honesty.

She opened to the first blank page.

Wrote: "Shoreline project. Erosion report ignored."

On the following line: "Capture of amendments 2025. Credit assigned to the office."

Then: "Marcos Aurélio doesn't read spreadsheets. He signs by shouting."

When he lifted his eyes, he felt that the air had changed in density.

— If I start this, there's no turning back.

Caio raised the glass.

— Welcome to the most interesting point of adult life.

Chapter 6: The Print Shop of the Invisible

The old print shop was on the east side of the city, in a warehouse hidden between a mechanic's workshop and a building materials depot. The original sign had fallen years ago; only the ghostly outline of the letters remained on the faded facade. Inside, the place smelled of old ink, damp paper, rust, and strong coffee. The kind of environment where political ideas seem more honest because they haven’t passed through marble.

Caio parked on the back street and turned off the car.

— Last chance to return to your controlled life and your built-in oven — he said.

Helena held the black notebook inside the bag.

— My built-in oven lost authority over me.

Arlete received them even before they entered. She was sixty-two years old, had very short gray hair, firm arms, and eyes that gave the impression of having already witnessed every possible form of political betrayal without being surprised. She wore a black t-shirt stained with paint and a red scarf around her neck.

— So this is the technique of Tourism — he said, without extending his hand.

Helena nodded.

— I am Helena.

— I know who you are. The question is who you decided to be today.

Inside, four other people looked up when they entered. A skinny young man with a rainbow pin on his jacket, a middle-aged woman with a document folder on her lap, an older man wearing a neighborhood association cap, and a public school teacher that Helena vaguely recognized from a city council hearing. No one smiled.

Caio placed a hand on her back and withdrew it when he felt her stiffness.

— Guys, this is Helena. Helena, this is Arlete, Lucas, Professor Berenice, Mr. João Batista, and Simone.

The name Simone hung in the air. The woman with the beige folder did not look at her directly. She had the kind of posture of someone who had learned to diminish her own presence to survive in hostile environments.

Arlete pointed to a chair.

— Sit.

Helena sat down. The wood creaked under her weight, although she was light.

— Caio told me that you want to "do the right thing," said Arlete. "I've heard that phrase from about thirty men before all of them voted with their pocket."

— I didn't come to ask for ready trust — Helena replied.

— Good thing.

Lucas leaned over the table, curious.

— So why did you come?

Helena opened the bag, took out the notebook, and placed it on the worn wood.

— Because I know how this city hall works from the inside. I know where the processes are deliberately blocked, I know who receives money without working, I know how the boardwalk project was set up to look big and generate contracts, not construction. I know where the money disappears before reaching your neighborhood. And I know exactly why Alberto keeps me in the background.

Arlete did not blink.

— Why?

— Because, if I step up a step, he loses the person who holds the building in place.

It was Simone who spoke for the first time:

— And why has that stopped being enough now?

The question came without aggression, but with insistence. Helena liked it for that.

— Because I realized that usefulness, when it has never been seen as recognition, is an elegant name for exploitation.

No one commented. She went on.

— Yesterday the mayor gave my promotion to the most incompetent secretary of the administration because he has 'image.' On the same day, my husband asked me for the car insurance and the lasagna for dinner as if I were his secretary. I think the two things finally came together inside me.

Lucas let out a brief laugh.

— What exactly happened to the lasagna?

— I didn't do it.

For the first time, Arlete showed something close to a smile.

— Now, this is political programming.

The ice decreased a little, but just a little.

Helena then opened the notebook and began to list facts. She didn't portray herself as a heroine, she didn't dramatize, she didn't ask for absolution. She exposed. The doctored erosion report. The advertising contracts that grew near an election year. The amendments drafted by her and announced as the result of "institutional coordination." The office's habit of hiding technical flaws at the last minute. The difficulty of tracking some of the payments from the Public Works Department. The pattern of pushing everything as an emergency when the interest was in bypassing bidding.

Simone raised her head at the word 'payments'.

— Do you have a test?

— Not yet on the financial side. But I have a history of procedures, minutes, emails, messages, and reports. And I have memory.

Arlete leaned back in the chair.

— Memory helps to write a speech. It does not help to face the Public Prosecutor's Office.

— So I haven't come to the Public Prosecutor's Office yet. I came to you.

— That's also not a small thing — said John the Baptist.

Berenice, the teacher, crossed her arms.

— What do you want from us?

Helena took half a second before responding. She needed to tell the right truth.

— I want to know if there is any way to turn what I know into something that serves those who have always worked for this city without participating in it.

Lucas whistled softly, impressed or worried.

Arlete grabbed a stack of papers from the table and aligned them slowly, a gesture of someone who organizes their thoughts with their hands.

— I will be clear. We know the machine from the outside. You know it from the inside. This can be useful.

Helena almost corrected the term, but she held herself back.

Arlete continued:

— The problem is that we've had insiders here before. It always ends the same way. The person arrives angry, uses our wear and tear as a stepping stone, makes a beautiful speech, and when the office door opens, starts talking about fiscal responsibility with the enemy's vocabulary.

Helena thought of Alberto, of Ricardo, of all the men who fed on ready-made vocabulary.

— If I turn this around, you will have the right to say it to my face.

— Telling it to your face is the least — Arlete replied. — My fear is that you won't return to where our face will be.

Silence again.

It was Caio who broke it.

— You always say that someone with technical weight and without ties is missing. She is that.

— And you always fall in love with causes that are too difficult — Arlete retorted.

— It's part of my charm.

Simone opened the folder she was holding in her lap, but didn't take anything out. She just touched the papers.

— If what she said about the waterfront is true, worse things are happening. There is an invoice at the Treasury that doesn't match the delivery. I didn't bring it because I haven't decided yet if I am willing to professionally ruin myself.

Helena turned to her.

— Don't do anything on my account.

— It wouldn't be because of you.

The sentence contained more story than explanation.

Arlete got up.

— No one is going to leave here with a grand promise today. We are going to observe. You are going to observe too. If you want to walk with us, start by listening. Saturday there is a meeting in Vila Esperança. No press. No posing. No savior.

Helena got up.

— I can hear.

Arlete moved closer until she was a few centimeters away from her.

— Listening is more difficult than speaking, technically. Especially when you have spent your entire life feeling responsible for solving everything.

Helena held the gaze.

— I know.

Arlete shook her head, as if noting the answer to assess later.

When Helena left the warehouse, the street air seemed different. Dirtier, more honest, more alive. Caio lit a cigarette that he didn't smoke, holding it between his fingers like a nervous accessory.

— So? — he/she asked.

Helena spent a few seconds looking at the facade of the print shop, at the rust, at the poorly lit street, at the unprecedented feeling of being in a place where no one wanted her to be efficient before wanting to understand her.

— So I think I spent many years confusing being needed with belonging.

Caio released the smoke.

— And now?

She held the strap of the bag more tightly.

— Now I want to find out the difference.

Interlude III: Three Dinners with Ricardo

The marriage didn't collapse all at once. It settled over small inclinations, like a building constructed on bad ground that seems firm for years until it starts showing cracks in the corners.

In the first year living together, Helena still confused comfort with partnership. Ricardo was attentive in public, remembered birthdays, bought wine for the two of them, praised her intelligence in front of friends. He also left the wet towel on the bed, forgot bills on the table, and considered it natural that she would know where any document he was looking for was.

On an ordinary Tuesday, shortly after the civil wedding, Ricardo arrived home early and found Helena finishing a presentation for the city hall at the table in the living room.

— You didn't make dinner? — he asked, dropping the bag on the couch.

— I'm closing this here. I'll do it in twenty minutes.

He opened the fridge, took out water, and said, without apparent malice:

— It's just that you organize yourself so well that I already go in hungry for ready-made food.

Helena laughed immediately. Only later would she realize how much that sentence had of a scheme.

In the third year, during a dinner with two couples of friends, the conversation turned to work and one of the men commented that he admired women "who can handle everything." Ricardo placed his hand on Helena's thigh under the table and said proudly:

— Mine is impressive. She solves everything without bothering me.

The others laughed. One of the women complimented her. Helena smiled out of politeness, chewing the anger as one chews cartilage. No one asked what exactly it meant to solve everything. No one found it strange that the main compliment was the absence of discomfort.

On the way back home, she commented:

— Did you notice that you talked about me as if I were a luxury appliance?

Ricardo was driving while looking at the empty avenue.

— Wow, Helena, you problematize everything. I was complimenting you.

She turned her face to the window and let the conversation die. The habit of not ruining the night was, by then, a second skin.

In sixth grade, when her mother was sick for a few weeks and Helena began to split her nights between the hospital, work, and the apartment, Ricardo sat at the table and asked the question that would follow her later like a cracked bell:

— Is there any chance this won't disrupt our routine so much?

She raised her eyes slowly.

— My mother is undergoing chemotherapy.

— I know. I'm just asking because my week is hellish and I can't live on improvisation.

That night, Helena left the kitchen, went to the bathroom, and cried silently sitting on the closed toilet. Not because of the isolated sentence. But because of the sudden understanding that, for Ricardo, real suffering only existed fully after going through the filter of its impact on him.

Even so, the next day, she prepared lunch boxes, got ahead with the laundry, and left notes on the fridge explaining where the dog's medications were.

A crack does not become a ruin the moment it appears. It becomes one when the person who sees it decides, out of exhaustion or hope, to pretend that the noise is from the wind.

Chapter 7: The Sandcastle

The City Hall's Grand Hall had a particular way of smelling of elegant lies. That morning, the freshly vacuumed carpet competed with the expensive cologne of the secretaries, the coffee served on silver trays, and the metallic anxiety of the cameras being set up. The men of the cabinet liked that environment because it made them seem bigger than they were. The yellowish light softened wrinkles, the marble echoed authority, and the chairs arranged in a semicircle gave the impression that everything there obeyed a very well-thought-out design.

Helena was backstage, tablet in hand, her hair tied in a bun so tight it pulled at her forehead. She had watched the entire presentation the night before and decided to make a single adjustment: not to hide the risk. The coastal erosion chart appeared in full. The corrective cost table, too. If Marcos Aurélio wanted to pose as the project leader, he would step on stage with the real ground beneath his feet.

Caio walked past her holding an extra microphone.

— Last chance to commit classic sabotage and cut the building's power — he murmured.

— Today I am going to commit something more offensive.

— What?

— Precision.

He smiled from the corner and went to adjust the transmission.

Alberto opened the press conference with the soft voice of the days of signing. He spoke about sustainable development, attracting investors, restoring the city's tourist appeal, urban integration. Marcos Aurélio, beside him, alternated small waves and broad smiles, as if he could already see himself on the next day's newspaper. When he took the microphone, he began going through the slides without looking at them, trusting his own repertoire of empty phrases.

— As you can see, the soil in the northern strip shows full viability for the implementation of the complex...

A journalist from the economics section interrupted him before he could finish.

— Secretary, the projected chart indicates a forty-two percent risk of erosion over the next five years. You just said 'full feasibility.' Which of the two pieces of information should the audience consider?

The entire hall held its breath.

Marcos looked at the screen, then at Alberto, then back at the screen. It was like watching a man trying to read his own name in an unknown language.

— Look, this is a technical index that...

— Which makes a heavy foundation in the area unfeasible, the secretary — the journalist insisted. — That's what's on the slide.

Alberto moved quickly. He knew how to improvise when disaster was still in its infancy.

— Helena, dear, can you come and explain the report better? — she called, in an overly sweet tone. — The technical team sometimes works with very conservative scenarios.

It was at that moment that the whole hall turned around.

Helena felt the focus of the lights touch her face even before taking the first step. It was always like that. When the invisible work failed to remain invisible, they called the woman in charge of saving it. The difference was that, this time, she was not willing to function as an emergency patch.

He walked to the pulpit.

— Good morning — he said, taking the microphone.

The timbre came out stable. The entire city, for the first time, would hear it without a filter.

— The index presented on the slide is not conservative. It is the most optimistic scenario available at this moment, already taking into account the containment works that fit within the current budget. The full report was delivered to the office yesterday at six seventeen p.m.

A reporter from the local radio leaned forward.

— So the project, as it stands, is not feasible?

Helena looked at Alberto. Then at Marcos. Then at the audience.

— As it stands, the project requires a thorough structural review before any deployment announcement.

Silence.

The word 'review' echoed on the marble more strongly than any scream. Marcos Aurélio opened his mouth, but nothing came out immediately. Alberto tried to reclaim the space.

— What Helena means...

— What I mean is in the report, Mayor — she interrupted him, still without raising her voice. — And the report is clear.

Caio, on the side of the room, lowered his eyes for a second to hide his smile.

The flashes began. Not because there was a show, but because the naked truth usually makes for a better photo than rhetoric. The press conference ended without a formal closing. Journalists stood up, assistants scrambled, someone turned off the projection too early, and Alberto approached Helena with the restrained expression of a man who knows he cannot shout in public.

— In my office. Now.

She stared at him for a second.

— I have a copy of the presentation to distribute to the press.

— Now, Helena.

He turned his back without waiting for an answer.

But Helena did not follow him. She went to the front row, handed three printed copies to reporters who were already asking for the material, and only then left the hall. She did not go to the office. She went to her own desk. She sat down, opened the drawer, separated personal documents, the laptop charger, a mug, two good pens, and the small succulent she had kept next to the monitor for three years.

Caio appeared at the door five minutes later.

— You realize that this was wonderful, right?

— It was also the end of my job.

— Sometimes it is the beginning of something else.

Helena closed the bag. The cell phone vibrated nonstop. Alberto. Chief of staff. Two unknown numbers. Ricardo.

Did not answer any.

He swiped his badge one last time at the turnstile as he left the building and felt a brief, almost physical dizziness. It was not fear of unemployment. It was something deeper: the shock of realizing that, for the first time in a long time, he had just chosen his own integrity over the maintenance of the system.

Outside, the late morning sun seemed excessively bright.

The castle had not fallen entirely.

But the first wall had cracked.

Chapter 8: The Night of the Suitcase

When Helena arrived at the apartment that afternoon, Ricardo already knew.

Not because she had told, but because, in medium-sized cities, politics runs through the air conditioning ducts. The video of the press conference was already circulating among groups of entrepreneurs, city councilors, secretaries' wives, and people who had never read a technical report, but loved to watch the exact moment when someone important lost control of the situation.

Ricardo was in the living room, standing, arms crossed, the television on a local news channel that repeated the same image: Helena at the microphone, face firm, the mayor frozen beside her.

— You’ve completely gone mad — those were his first words.

Helena closed the door slowly.

— Good afternoon to you too.

— No kidding. You humiliated the mayor in public.

— No. I read the report in public.

— It's the same.

She set the bag down on the sideboard and took off her shoes. She wanted to avoid the feeling of arguing on a stage.

— Maybe it works for you.

Ricardo ran his hand through his hair.

— Do you have any idea what you did to your career?

— I have.

— And what about our life?

Helena raised her eyes.

— What exactly in our life depends on me lying for incompetent men?

He let out a dry laugh.

— Don't play innocent. Alberto opened doors for you. Gave you stability when you were broken.

The sentence came as it had since 2018: not as a memory, but as a leash.

— I worked ten times more than the position required, Ricardo.

— No one is denying that.

— I am, yes. Every time you call my work help. Every time you say I was protected. Every time you act as if I should be eternally grateful for not having sunk alone.

Ricardo took a deep breath, controlling his voice.

— The problem is that you want to turn everything into oppression. Not every frustration is structural injustice, Helena. Sometimes life just requires patience.

She walked to the kitchen and turned on the tap to fill a glass of water. She needed some simple action to avoid responding in the same tone.

— Do you know what impresses me the most? — he said without turning around. — It's that you really think you are being reasonable.

— Because I am.

Helena drank half of the glass and rested both hands on the sink.

— Did Alberto call?

— He/She/It called.

— And did you answer?

— I answered because someone in this house still thinks about the future.

She turned around slowly.

— And what did he say?

— That, if you apologize, it’s still possible to hold off on the formal dismissal. Come back as an assistant, stay quieter for a while, let the dust settle.

Helena felt a fatigue so vast that it almost merged with lucidity.

— You negotiated my submission without talking to me.

— I negotiated a possible exit.

— For whom?

Ricardo opened his arms.

— For us.

The word no longer made any sense.

At that moment, Helena realized that Ricardo's 'we' had always meant a very specific arrangement: him at the center of stability, her in the work of sustaining it. Everything that deviated from that seemed to him like deviation, lack of control, irresponsibility.

She went to the bedroom, pulled a carry-on suitcase from under the bed, and opened it on the bedspread. Ricardo came behind her.

— What are you doing?

— Going away.

— Where to?

— I still don't know.

— Don't be childish.

Helena took clothes from the wardrobe with economical, almost serene movements. Two pairs of pants, three shirts, underwear, the laptop charger, document folder, black notebook, medicines, the last folder of the renegotiated debt, the passport she hardly used.

— It is childish for an adult man not to know where his own bills are and to call that a partnership.

Ricardo held the handle of the suitcase before she closed it.

— Are you going to throw away eight years because of an ego crisis?

She lifted her eyes.

— You still think this is about ego.

— And what is it about? About the lasagna? About a position?

— About me no longer being able to live as if my best use were to serve.

He dropped the suitcase, but didn't move out of the way.

— And what are you going to do? Sleep on Caio's couch? Become a political meme? Think that these people are going to carry you in their arms?

Helena thought about the print shop, about Arlete, about Lucas, about Simone clutching the folder to her chest. She thought about her own house and how, suddenly, nothing there seemed like a shelter to her.

— Maybe I'll sleep on Caio's couch. Maybe I'll become a meme. Maybe I'll find out too late that I'm wrong. But staying here just to keep your idea of order is no longer an option.

Ricardo lost the measure of his own face for a second.

— I helped you rebuild everything.

— No. You turned my debt into currency of obedience.

He looked at her as if he had never heard her speak at that moment.

Helena closed the suitcase. She went to the kitchen, turned off the coffee machine, picked up the laptop from the table, and wrote down the insurance broker's number on a piece of paper.

He left the note next to the fruit bowl.

— The broker attends until six - he said. "You can do it."

He/She opened the door.

Outside, the building's hallway smelled of disinfectant and wet plants. Caio was leaning against the elevator, as if he had sensed the exact moment of her exit.

— I brought the car — he said.

Helena nodded.

As he crossed the gate, he did not feel triumph. He felt loss. The mourning for a life that, although narrow, was still a known life. But he also felt something else, more discreet and deeper: space.

When the building's elevator door closed behind her, the sound seemed like that of a vault door opening from the inside.

Chapter 9: The Politics of the Wastebasket

Vila Esperança was less than twenty minutes from downtown, but the political distance was another matter. The asphalt ended early, the sewage appeared whenever it wanted, the houses leaned on each other like tired relatives, and the garbage trucks passed with a humiliating regularity: punctual during the councilman's visiting weeks, erratic the rest of the time.

Helena arrived in Caio's car's passenger seat, wearing sneakers, jeans, and a white t-shirt, with her hair tied in a simple ponytail. It wasn't a costume of humility; it was her at her fullest without armor. Even so, she felt out of place as she stepped onto the residents' association court, where about forty people were waiting for her with the cautious look of those who had already been the object of too many promises.

Arlete was in the center of the space with her arms crossed.

— Here no one wants a prepared speech — he warned. — They want to be listened to.

Helena nodded.

The first words came sharply, as was only fair. A mother asked why the health center never had enough gauze. A motorcycle taxi driver wanted to know why the asphalt ran out two streets before his house. An older woman, Dona Nair, asked if that 'lady from the television' knew how much a gas cylinder cost.

Helena listened to everything standing, without trying to fix reality with quick phrases. When finally Arlete made a gesture with her chin, allowing her to speak, she did not take out a notebook, did not open a presentation, did not start with the edge.

— I spent ten years doing the work of men who appeared in the photo in my place — I said. — And at home, I spent years organizing the life of a man who thought this was natural. On Tuesday, I looked at an eggplant lasagna and realized that I was using all my energy to keep standing worlds that didn’t see me. Maybe this seems small compared to what you live. But it was the first time I understood, in my body, that the logic is the same.

People didn't applaud. What happened was better. They stopped looking at her as if they were looking at an employee on a visit.

A young woman in her early twenties, with a boy in her arms, asked:

— And what does that change for us?

Helena breathed.

— It changes if I can use what I know to open the city's budget and show why the money doesn't reach here. It changes if you agree not to treat me as a savior and treat me as someone who has the obligation to be accountable.

Lucas, sitting on a low wall at the back of the court, was the first to smile.

At the end of the meeting, Dona Nair approached with a twenty-real bill folded four times.

— I don't have a party — she said. — I'm angry. Does that work?

Helena tried to refuse.

— No need.

— Yes, she does need to. It's the first time I've seen a woman speak this way without apologizing in the middle.

Helena carefully took the note. That money was worth less for its value than for what it carried: a shift of trust.

On the way back to the printing shop, with the note kept inside the black notebook, she realized that the city she knew from the office window was just a silent drawing. The real city had mud on its sneakers, a shopping bag on its arm, and precise memory of those who had ignored it.

Chapter 10: Good Girls Don't Make Noise

On the first night sleeping on Caio's couch, Helena couldn't turn off her body. The apartment was small, with books piled on the floor, a huge fern in the living room, and an honest mess that no one there tried to turn into a moral lesson. Still, at two in the morning, she was already picking up a mug left in the sink, straightening a crooked cushion, folding a fallen blanket.

Caio appeared at the bedroom door, disheveled.

— If you start organizing my house to thank for hospitality, I will charge emotional rent.

Helena let the blanket drop on the armchair.

— I couldn't sleep.

He watched her for a moment.

— You also can't stay still.

It wasn't criticism. It was a diagnosis.

When Caio went back to sleep, Helena was left alone in the dark room, listening to the hum of the refrigerator. And, for the first time in a long time, she let her memory go further back than 2018.

She saw the kitchen of her childhood, her mother sewing until late, her father coming home tired and silent, her younger brother asking for help with his homework. She saw her fourth-grade teacher praising her handwriting, her discipline, her readiness to explain exercises to slower classmates. "Helena is an angel," they said. "Helena is no trouble." "Helena helps with everything."

In adolescence, while her friends were learning to slam doors, Helena had learned to anticipate conflict. She brought home a good report card and immediately washed the dishes afterward, so she wouldn't seem proud. At her first internship, at eighteen, she put together the presentation that her boss presented as if it were his own and heard from an older colleague that it was normal, that a smart girl doesn't pick a fight too early. At the next job, she stayed late for three weeks in a row to save a poorly planned event and received as a reward a 'you can handle a task like no one else'.

Good. Helpful. Mature. Organized. Reliable.

The compliments changed clothes, but they always asked the same thing: that she turn her own existence into support.

Around four in the morning, Helena opened the black notebook and wrote on a loose sheet: 'I was not born useful. I was trained.'

It was the harshest sentence she had ever written about herself.

The next morning, Caio left coffee ready and a note on the counter:

"If you want to run away, let me know. If you want to stay, there's a meeting at ten. In both cases, please do not organize my cutlery drawer."

Helena read the note twice. She smiled. Then she put it in her purse without really understanding why.

She still did not know what she would do with her life. She did not know if the party would actually give her a genuine nomination, if Alberto would crush her before that, if Ricardo would turn the separation into public blackmail, if next month's bills would fit in the account. But she knew one thing that, until the previous week, she did not know how to feel:

The fear was no longer enough to return her to the usual place.

Chapter 11: The Lasagna Video

Caio insisted that the first public response could not be a programmatic manifesto. It had to be a short, direct video, without a marketing look.

— If you speak like a technician before speaking like a person, they win — she explained, placing the cellphone on an improvised tripod over stacks of books. — The city needs to understand why a seemingly controlled woman decided to blow up a lasagna and a waterfront project in the same week.

Helena thought the sentence was terrible. Then she realized that it worked.

She recorded sitting at the kitchen table in Caio's apartment, in front of a wall that was too white. She wore no makeup except for lip balm and was not holding any paper. She talked about the denied position, about the press conference, about the night she realized that work and marriage fed on the same mechanism. She did not dramatize. She also did not apologize.

— The problem is not the lasagna — she said to the camera. — The problem is when a woman’s entire life comes to be treated as silent infrastructure. At the city hall, I organized the work for men to appear. At home, I organized life for a man to rest. When I stopped serving in silence, both worlds got upset on the same day.

Caio did not ask for a second take.

He posted the video shortly before lunch with a dry caption: 'She didn't freak out. She realized.'

The first reactions came slowly. A comment here, another there, timid shares, two private messages from former college classmates saying 'thank you for speaking up.' Then, in less than an hour, the algorithm found the right crack. The video entered groups of mothers, teachers, civil servants, shopkeepers, nurses, divorced women, married women wanting to be less married than they were. When Helena opened her phone again, there were hundreds of notifications.

In the middle of them, a Pix of twelve reais.

Sender: Maria das Dores S.

Message: "It's little, but it's mine. Just so it doesn't look like only rich men can afford it, you know."

Helena stared at the screen in silence. Twelve reais. Less than an executive lunch combo in the downtown area. More than any abstract applause.

In the afternoon, there had already been seventy-three small transfers, none over fifty reais. Dona Nair had sent a photo of the twenty-reais note stuck to the fridge with a saint magnet and the caption: 'now it was through Pix because my grandson taught me.'

When the mayor decided to respond, he chose the tone that men like him reserve for women who escape control: paternalism with a veneer of concern.

— Helena is a great technician, but she is clearly going through a moment of personal instability — he said in an interview. — I sincerely hope she takes care of herself.

Caio turned off the television halfway through.

— If I hear 'personal instability' one more time, I'm going to tattoo 'electoral crime' on his forehead.

Helena, however, was no longer listening to Alberto like before. There was too much noise coming from somewhere else. Ordinary people saying 'me too,' 'it happened to me,' 'my boss does that,' 'my husband calls it help,' 'I thought I was exaggerating,' 'now I see that I wasn't.'

At the end of the night, she opened the bank app once more.

The amount raised didn’t even pay for a decent piece of campaign.

Paid something better.

It proved that there were people willing to put small amounts of money into the possibility of a woman not asking for permission to exist.

Chapter 12: Simone at the Finance Department

Simone had been working on the fourth floor of the Department of Finance for eleven years and had learned, during that time, to master a specific art: the art of appearing less intelligent than she was so that insecure men would not make her a target. She spoke quietly in meetings, brought her lunch in discreet containers, avoided correcting the secretary in public, and kept copies of reports that someone always asked to be 'adjusted later' in a personal folder inside her backpack.

In the week of the lasagna video, she recognized Helena on the screen before recognizing herself in the discomfort. What struck her was not the story about the kitchen, but the phrase about silent infrastructure. Simone thought about her own department, the way she closed spreadsheets and untangled budget hacks so that incompetent men could sign the final piece smiling. She thought about her immediate boss who told her not to pick fights, the secretary who called her 'our little calculator,' the leftover overpricing from the shoreline project that passed across her screen like persistent flies.

On Thursday, when he arrived at work, he found on his desk two commitment notes with different amounts for the same earthmoving service. The supplier was an old acquaintance of Alberto's office. The final destination of the fund, the project that never started in the Vila Esperança section.

Simone printed the two documents. Then she printed the approval chain. Then she took a deep breath in front of the hot machine, feeling her heart beat in her neck. If she took that to the management, it would disappear. If she kept it to herself, she would continue participating in the mechanism. If she handed it outside, she would lose the false calm of the coming years.

At lunchtime, he sent a message to Caio from a number he didn't recognize.

I have stuff from the coastline. If she is serious, I will also have to be.

The meeting was scheduled in a small park, near the bus terminal, in the late afternoon. Simone arrived earlier, holding the briefcase like someone holding a restless animal. When Helena appeared in jeans and a light blue shirt, without elaborate makeup and with shoulders still tense from someone who hasn't unlearned war, Simone felt a strange relief. The woman from the video seemed like a real person.

— I saw your video three times before sending a message," Simone said, bluntly. "The third time I was angry because I realized that I had already understood all that years ago and I just hadn't named it.

Helena did not interrupt her.

Simone delivered the folder.

— Here there is a duplicate invoice, a suspicious addendum, and a trace of overpricing that doesn't match the material delivered. It's not everything. It's enough to show that the waterfront construction and the abandonment of Vila Esperança speak the same language.

Helena opened the folder with reverent care. The papers were dry, technical, almost bloodless. But there was blood in them. Blood of people who were left without streets, without drainage, without daycare.

— You know the risk of this — said Helena.

— I know.

— And even so, he/she brought it.

Simone let out a short, bitter laugh.

— I also make lasagna, Helena. Mine only comes in the form of a spreadsheet and a budget.

They talked for almost an hour. Simone explained the flow of money, the bureaucratic shortcuts, the internal nicknames for the contracts, the way the department called a 'fix' what in any serious country would be called fraud. Helena listened as she had in Vila Esperança: without taking the other's story for herself, without turning someone else's pain into personal fuel before understanding it.

When they said goodbye, Simone still seemed scared.

— I am not as brave as you think — she confessed.

Helena closed the folder.

— Neither do I.

— So why does it seem?

— Because I got tired of seeming obedient.

That night, back at the print shop, Helena spread the documents on the large table. Arlete read in silence. Caio walked back and forth like an electric animal. Lucas photographed everything to create a secure file.

— "This changes the game — said Arlete, finally."

— "Things change if we know how to play — replied Helena."

She didn't sleep. Between one spreadsheet and another, an idea took shape with increasing clarity: it was no longer enough to denounce. It was necessary to offer an alternative design for the city.

For the first time, the candidacy stopped feeling like an abstraction.

It came to seem like a consequence.

Chapter 13: The Divorce of Expectations

Ricardo appeared at the print shop on a Saturday morning, when the sun was already passing through the high tiles and marking the cement floor with oblique streaks of light. He was without a tie, wearing a light shirt and sunglasses, as if he had chosen a reasonable man's outfit for a difficult conversation. Helena saw him get out of the car through the side window and felt, before anything else, pity for the old version of herself who would have rushed to ease his discomfort.

Arlete lifted her eyes from the mimeographed newspaper she was reviewing.

— Do you want me to kick the man out?

— Not yet.

Ricardo entered looking around with mild disgust. The disorder of the print shop offended him more than an open-air sewer would, because the disorder of the poor did not fit into the safe aesthetics of his world.

— Can we talk alone? — he asked.

— What you have to say can be said here.

He cast a quick glance at Caio and Arlete.

— Helena.

— Here.

Ricardo took a deep breath.

— I came because this situation has gone too far. Your video is everywhere. My dad keeps commenting on it. At work, people have already come to ask me if you really went crazy or if it's a strategy.

— And what did you answer?

— That you are going through a phase.

Caio let out an indignant noise through his nose, but held back.

Ricardo continued:

— Alberto is still willing to handle this discreetly. If you end this exposure, we can contain the damage. You go back to the city hall, let these people use someone else as a showcase, and we reorganize the house.

Helena felt the old mechanism reveal itself piece by piece. For Ricardo, the problem had never been injustice. It had been embarrassment.

— Did you come to pick me up or did you come to take the life I was organizing for you?

He got offended even before responding.

— I came to try to save what still remains.

— Save what?

— Our wedding.

Helena took two steps to the table, rested the tips of her fingers on the wood, and spoke with a calmness that irritated him more than any scream.

— Our marriage ended when I realized that you called a whole routine a partnership in which I thought for both of us.

Ricardo took off his glasses, as if he needed his whole face to support the argument.

— This is unfair.

— Unfair was you meeting with the mayor to negotiate my silence.

— I was trying to prevent you from destroying your own life.

— My life wasn't that order in which I did everything and still thanked you for not leaving.

He opened his mouth, closed it, looked for another entrance.

— You have always been intense. After 2018 that got worse. I just tried to provide stability.

Helena nodded slowly.

— That's the point, Ricardo. You think you gave me stability. You don't realize you offered me a nice enclosure.

For a second, he seemed genuinely lost. That hurt her more than if he had been cruel on purpose. It would be simpler to hate him entirely. But Ricardo was the type of man mass-produced by a culture that calls male comfort normality.

— So that's it? — he asked. — Are you going to throw everything away because of a campaign that doesn't even exist?

— I am not throwing myself in the trash. I am stopping living inside it.

Arlete put down the newspaper. Caio pretended to fiddle with his phone, but he was smiling inwardly.

Ricardo put his glasses back on, defeated in appearance and irritated in substance.

— When this blows up, don't look for me.

Helena did not answer immediately.

— I spent years not looking for myself, Ricardo. That was long enough.

He left without slamming the door, which somehow seemed more final.

Helena was silent for a few seconds. Then she exhaled and sat down.

Arlete pushed a cup of coffee to her.

— It hurts more when the prison used a polite tone — he said.

Helena took a sip.

— It hurts.

— But now it gave a name.

She nodded.

On the table next to it, Simone's folder remained open.

Outside, the city looked exactly the same as the day before.

It's just that Helena was no longer there.

Chapter 14: The Plan of the Invisible Women

It was Lucas who suggested the name on a sultry afternoon when six people were talking at the same time about drainage, daycare, public lighting, bus fares, and budget transparency.

— It’s no use calling this a traditional government program — he said, leaning on the windowsill of the printing house. — This here is a survival plan with a budget.

Caio lifted his eyes from the computer.

— Marketing-wise terrible. Politically excellent.

Arlete knocked the pencil on the table.

— Name.

Lucas thought for two seconds.

— Plan of the Invisibles.

No one contested.

In the following two weeks, the print shop turned into a city workshop. Helena moved among makeshift tables with maps, spreadsheets, and scribbled sheets. Berenice coordinated a listening session with mothers from the public network about daycare and food. João Batista gathered demands for drainage and land regularization. Lucas translated into simple language what the team discussed in technical terms. Simone, still working at the Farm during the day, arrived at night with numbers and left before midnight, haunted and determined.

Helena discovered that she knew how to lead without repeating the pattern of the men she had met. Not because she was naturally better, but because she paid attention to the cost of each decision on those who were already carrying too much weight.

On a Wednesday, after an especially long listening circle with lunch ladies, housewives, community agents, and caregivers of the elderly, Dona Odete appeared for the first time at the print shop. She carried a large bag and a small presence that made the environment lower its tone a bit.

— I don't stay up late — he warned, sitting at the end of the table. — But I came to say something. At city hall, the important people think we don't listen. We listen. And we take notes.

She took out of her bag a little school notebook full of dates, schedules, and incomplete sentences.

— I don’t know how to speak beautifully — he said. — But I know the moment when each secretary started treating school as an expense and lunch as a favor.

Helena received the notebook as one receives a relic.

That night ended with the first consolidated version of the plan: complete review of the waterfront, citizen audit of emergency contracts, readable transparency portal, expansion of daycare centers, peripheral drainage program, public procurement with open tracking, shelter for women, municipal policy to combat LGBTphobia, recovery of school meals with local production.

When the meeting emptied, Caio was left alone with Helena in front of the whiteboard filled with arrows, numbers, and neighborhood names.

— Do you realize that this is already a campaign? — he asked.

Helena ran her fingers through the list of priorities.

— I realize that this here is already a better city than the one that exists.

— The party will formalize the invitation tomorrow.

She closed her eyes for a moment. She felt fear, exhaustion, grief, a desire to retreat. She also felt the light weight of Maria das Dores's twelve reais, Dona Nair's bill, Dona Odete's notebook, Simone's folder, Caio's sofa, Arlete's phrase about going back to where the faces were.

— I accept — I said, before I had time to break down courage into sensible arguments.

Caio smiled without irony.

— So we will need more coffee.

At the exit, Helena stood for a moment at the door of the print shop, looking at the almost empty street. For the first time in many years, she had no idea what the next month would be like.

Curiously, that seemed less like a threat than a beginning.

Interlude IV: The First Little Cow

The first meeting after the candidacy was formalized was an honest disaster to the extent that Helena left it with more confidence than she would have from a flawless meeting. The table at the print shop was covered with notebooks, an old laptop, a thermos, a calculator without batteries, campaign flyers from the previous election reused on the back, and a budget that barely allowed for printing hope on plain paper.

Caio opened the conversation with numbers.

— If we spend everything we have today, we can pay for water, internet, three banners, and one hundred and fifty flyers with decent paper weight. If we want luxury, we will have to choose between the campaign and electricity.

Lucas raised his hand like a diligent student.

— I vote for light. Campaigning in the dark gets too conceptual.

No one laughed much. It was true.

The party representative, a smooth-spoken man named Fábio, showed up twenty minutes late and spent the entire meeting trying to convince the team that the campaign needed to "mature Helena's image."

— The lasagna piece was good for going viral — he said. — But now we need to move the candidate to something more institutional. Less cooking, less personal pain, more management.

Arlete answered before Helena.

— The problem is that a man thinks the kitchen is a detail when he has spent his whole life eating.

Fábio smiled like someone who tolerates folklore.

— I am talking about serious strategy.

Helena looked at him and realized that this would be a parallel battle of the campaign: defending not only the candidacy but also its grammar.

— A serious strategy, for me, is not to uproot the campaign from the place where it was born," he said. "I'm not going to become a neutral version of myself to reassure columnists.

Fábio wrote down something that he certainly considered a 'problem of handling'.

The meeting worsened when the topic turned to money. The party promised very little and it was still conditioned on initial performance. Two businessmen sympathetic to the independent opposition had backed out after the operation at Obras. A state representative liked the speech but wanted to wait for a 'signal of feasibility.' In practice, the campaign was starting as almost all things that truly threaten order start: poor.

In the middle of the discussion, Caio's cell phone beeped.

— Another Pix — he said, looking at the screen.

— How much? — asked Lucas.

— Four reais and fifty cents.

Caio showed the associated message.

"Sorry it's little. It's the money for today's bread, but I have flour at home."

The silence that came afterwards was not embarrassment. It was respect.

Throughout that afternoon, nineteen more small transfers arrived: eight reais, twelve, two ninety, seventeen, five. Along with them, messages too short to fit sophisticated analysis and too strong to be treated as decoration: 'Go for us,' 'Don't let go,' 'First time I believe,' 'My husband laughed, so I sent it secretly,' 'If it gets lost, at least someone spoke.'

Helena ran the tip of her finger across the screen without actually touching anything.

— Fábio, did you understand? — he asked, without taking his eyes off the cellphone.

— What?

— The campaign is already paid in the place that matters. The rest we build.

The man opened a smile without conviction.

— It's nice. It just doesn't replace structure.

Arlete lifted her head.

— No. But it reminds [you] of the candidate she cannot become.

That night, when the meeting ended, Helena was alone for a few minutes with the spreadsheet open. The amount raised was ridiculous by any traditional calculation. And yet, it seemed larger than many fat checks she had seen circulate in an office. It didn't come as a favor expecting a return. It came from scarcity turned into a bet.

She wrote at the top of the spreadsheet:

"No small real is small when it buys voice."

Part II: The Making of Helena the Protagonist

Chapter 15: The Launch

The launch of the candidacy took place at Peace Square, in the heart of Vila Esperança, and that in itself was already a political gesture. The party had suggested the auditorium of a hotel downtown, with air conditioning, an acrylic podium, and upholstered chairs. Helena refused. She wanted the campaign to be born where the city had been trained not to look.

The 'stage' was the cargo bed of a freight truck. The banners had been hand-painted by women from the neighborhood. Instead of receptionists in heels, there were teenagers organizing water in cooler boxes and men dragging plastic chairs. Caio coordinated the live broadcast with a headset in one ear and a nervous glow on his face. Lucas handed out leaflets with a QR code for the Invisible Plan. Arlete pretended to be calm.

Before going up, Helena stayed a few seconds behind the canvas, listening to the noise of the square. Pots clanged rhythmically somewhere in the distance. Children ran around. An accordion insisted on surviving amidst the commotion. It did not resemble the solemnities of the office at all, and precisely because of that, it seemed more serious.

— There's time to run away through the back street — murmured Caio.

— Would you leave me?

— Never. I just think it's important to preserve the illusion of freedom.

She laughed, took a deep breath, and went up.

The noise grew before the first word. It wasn't a huge crowd, but it was a lively crowd. People there because they wanted to be. People who hadn't been brought by a union-rented bus or by the promise of a raffle. People who wanted to see with their own eyes if the lasagna woman could bear the weight of her own gesture.

Helena held the microphone with both hands at the beginning, to stabilize her body.

— I spent years believing that my place was to do the work and disappear behind it — she said. — At the city hall, I wrote projects that came out of men's mouths as if they were theirs. At home, I organized entire days so that my effort seemed natural. I learned very early to be useful. And very late to realize the cost of it.

She told enough of her story to not become a confession. She spoke of the city captured by the same names, of the technique used as servitude, of the politics that call what is exploitation help. When she raised the black notebook and Dona Odete's notebook together, the gesture was simple and precise: a life behind the scenes meeting another.

— I am not a candidate to become a decorative exception — she said. — I am a candidate because this city was built on the backs of people who were never called to the table. And because, if real work mattered more than a last name, half of the power in this city would have already changed hands.

At the end of the speech, a short summer rain began to fall over the square. No one ran. People pulled out plastic bags, covered the speakers, protected the children, and stayed. Helena stayed too. Wet, with her hair sticking to the nape of her neck, she saw Dona Nair beat the pot harder and understood that the launch had been made.

A good campaign is not born at the moment someone advertises.

It is born when others decide not to budge.

Chapter 16: The Blow to the Past

Two days later, the counterattack came.

The city's largest portal featured on the morning headline: 'Candidate of the rupture hides millionaire bankruptcy.' Below, documents from 2018, protest photos of the title, amount of debts, name of the former company. There was no objective falsehood. There was intention.

Helena read everything while sitting at the printing shop table, as silence settled around.

— They will make it look like you are irresponsible with money," said Simone, already calculating the political damage. "They will say that those who broke their own company cannot touch the public budget.

Caio seemed about to walk through the wall.

— And they even leaked things from a finished case. This came from inside.

Helena felt her body recoil to an old version of herself. The old shame still knew the way. For a short and dangerous moment, she wanted to disappear. She wanted to go back to being a faceless technician. She wanted to apologize for having failed in front of people who never paid a supplier with their own wedding ring.

It was Arlete who interrupted the reflection.

— They are using your scar as if it were proof of guilt — he said. — Decide quickly whether you will hide it or turn it into a weapon. The middle ground only serves a powerful man.

Helena asked for half an hour alone.

He sat at the back of the print shop with the documents spread on the floor. He read the name of his own company, remembered the notary, the stacked boxes, the rain on the day of the execution. But he also remembered the rest. The fraudulent contract rejected. The salaries paid before his own rent. The refusal to falsify a note to please Varella. The cost of not playing the game.

When he returned to the front, he already knew what he would do.

He/She/They recorded a video in one take.

— They leaked my past because they believe that shame is a good instrument of governance — I said to the camera. — I went bankrupt in 2018. I went bankrupt because I refused to commit a fraud that would have saved my cash and tainted my signature. I paid every cent I owed. I worked in silence to get back on my feet. Those who have never seen an overdue bill might think that disqualifies me. I think the opposite. I know the weight of every real because I’ve had to choose between rent and payroll. The mayor is not afraid of my bankruptcy. He is afraid of the fact that I survived it without selling out.

The video came out late in the afternoon.

That night, the hashtag was not about ruin.

It was about reconstruction.

Chapter 17: The Low Blow

Since bankruptcy did not bring her down, they tried another path: morality.

A report full of insinuations began to circulate on blogs funded by official advertising. Photos of Helena entering Caio's building late at night, headlines about "abandonment of the home," malicious questions about her relationship with a gay man, comments about "family values" and "inappropriate influences" within the campaign. There was no direct accusation. There was enough mud to force the victim to prove her innocence.

Lucas arrived at the print shop with a hard face.

— They already took a screenshot of my profile and are sending it in my aunt's church group — I said. — They said the campaign wants to turn the city into an ideological laboratory.

Caio closed the laptop forcefully.

— They can't stand that we exist without shame.

Helena looked from one to the other and realized that the attack, this time, was not aimed at her alone. It targeted everyone around her and escaped the acceptable costume for the conservative façade city.

— The answer is not going to be defensive — he said.

In that night's live stream, Helena spoke alongside Caio and Lucas, without sidelining either of them in the name of electoral calculation.

— The mayor is very worried about who comes in and out of my campaign coordinator's house — he began. — Maybe because he has never been concerned about who comes in and out of the city's contracts. I am not going to apologize for having friends, for leaving a bad marriage, or for working with people that this city has gotten used to pushing aside. The family that matters to politics is not the picture-perfect one. It is the woman who takes two buses to work, the boy who gets beaten at school just for existing, the grandmother who raises her grandchild alone, the couple who supports their household by selling lunch boxes. What they call a deviation, I call real life.

It was not a comfortable night.

But in the morning, the symbolic hug at the print shop had more people than the team could count without making a mistake.

Chapter 18: The First Threat

The threat arrived on plain paper, inside a brown envelope with no sender, left in the early hours under the rolling door of the print shop.

When Arlete opened it early in the morning, she found only three typed lines:

Go back to the kitchen. Next time the city might not be so patient. We know where your mother lives.

Helena read the note once. Then she folded it with excessive care, as if the gesture could reduce the content. She felt cold in her hands in a thirty-two degree heat.

Up to that point, the campaign had an abstract risk. Attack in the press, venomous comment, phone ringing late at night. That paper changed in nature. Fear, when it gets an address, ceases to be a metaphor.

— Let's file a police report and take Mrs. Celina out of the house for a few days — Caio said immediately.

Helena nodded, but her body took time to follow. Part of her resisted the idea of moving her mother because of a choice of her own. Another part, more honest, already knew that the price of courage did not come in installments.

At night, after spending hours between the police station, phone calls, and the embarrassment of explaining the threat to Celina, Helena was left alone in the printing shop's bathroom, looking at her own face in the cracked mirror above the stone sink. It was a bad, crooked mirror, incapable of flattering any face. Still, it gave her something that the city hall mirror had never given back: exhaustion without makeup and fear without shame.

Arlete appeared at the door.

— Want to give up? — he/she asked.

Helena took a long time to respond.

— Today I wanted.

Arlete nodded as one who approves the truth, not the decision.

— I also wanted in every fight that was worth it.

Helena pressed her forehead against the cold mirror for a second.

— And why do we keep going?

Arlete crossed her arms.

— Because, if they can even choose how far we go through fear, then the government is already theirs before the ballot box.

That night, Helena did not sleep. She sat on Caio's sofa listening to the building's elevator, the noise of the street, her heart out of rhythm. At four in the morning, she opened the black notebook and wrote just one line:

"Courage is not the absence of fear. It is the refusal to obey."

Interlude VII: Celina Out of the House

Convincing Celina to leave the house for a few days was harder than reporting the threat to the police station.

— I am not going to run away because of a cowardly man — she said, sitting on the edge of the bed with the bag already open and all her stubbornness in her chin.

Helena was standing in front of the wardrobe, sorting clothes for her mother to spend a few days at a cousin's house in another city.

— It's not an escape. It's caution.

— Too much caution also becomes a half-lived life.

Helena closed her eyes for a moment. The urge to respond as a daughter, not as a mayor or responsible adult, almost came. Instead, she sat beside her mother.

— I know that you don't want to give me this job.

Celina turned her face to her.

— That's the problem. You keep thinking that any care the other person receives from you becomes your work.

The sentence hit hard because it came from the person who, unintentionally, had helped to found this mechanism.

— You called me a nuisance my whole life — said Helena, more quietly.

Celina fell silent. Then she ran her hand over her knee, an old gesture from when she used to think before sewing words.

— I called because I was tired, daughter. Not because I wanted you to become anyone's bed.

Helena felt the crying rise from a place too old to be contained with elegance.

— Sometimes I think I learned everything wrong.

— You didn't learn everything wrong. You learned too early to patch up the world. Now you are learning to choose what deserves mending.

The two sat in silence for a few seconds, listening to the bad fan in the room and the noise of the street coming through the window.

— Are you afraid? — asked Celina.

Helena breathed through her nose.

— Very.

— Great.

She let out a nervous laugh.

— Great?

— A dangerous man is the one who loses his fear of himself. A woman who is not too afraid is usually very tired or very lying. You're scared because you know the size of what you're facing. This is health, not weakness.

When they finally went out with the suitcase, Celina stopped at the door of the house and looked back. Not like someone saying goodbye forever, but like someone gauging the dignity of doing what is necessary even without liking it.

In the car, already on the way to the bus station, he took his daughter's hand.

— If you step back, I keep loving you. If you step forward, too. But don't shrink yourself again to make any man's job easier. That, I can't stand to see again.

Helena looked out the window at the darkening city.

That night, for the first time, he understood that his mother was not just the source of part of his training.

She was also the harshest witness to his attempt to unlearn it.

Chapter 19: The Army of Ants

The nickname was born from a joke by Caio and stuck because it fit. "Army of Ants" was the name the team started giving to the group of people who kept the city hall running without ever appearing on a nice organizational chart: kitchen staff, receptionists, drivers, IT interns, maintenance personnel, cleaning assistants, orderlies, typists, night watchmen.

Mrs. Odete was the general who never asked for a rank.

It was she who warned that Marcos Aurélio's office shredder was working overtime. It was she who managed to find out on which days the drivers received contradictory orders. It was she who managed to get the strips of paper of what they tried to destroy to the printing house, inside a clean garbage bag.

Lucas and two computer science students spent an entire night putting pages back together. Simone cross-checked dates. Helena recognized signatures, initials, dispatch formats, language shortcuts. The puzzle gave back to the world what the office preferred to see as confetti: suspicious addenda, verbal orders confirmed by email, technical corrections ignored with margin notes, explicit attempts to 'mask' risk so as not to scare investors.

Meanwhile, the city hall building itself began to feel Helena's absence as a physical symptom. Passwords took longer to be reset. Official cars 'lost' their way. The mayor's office air conditioner broke down on the hottest day of the month. Urgent documents vanished between one floor and another. The machine didn't stop. It only lost the illusion that it worked on its own.

On Friday night, an IT intern appeared with a USB drive in his pocket and his hand shaking.

— I did mirroring before they formatted the Obras server — he said. — If I lose the internship, at least I lose knowing why.

Helena held the device as if she were holding a match near gasoline.

There was no turning back.

Interlude V: Dona Odete Was Not Furniture

Mrs. Odete had worked at the city hall for twenty-seven years. She had started as a cleaning assistant when Alberto was still just a promising councilman and the main building smelled more of leaks than imported perfume. She had seen five mayors, three administrative reforms, two waves of outsourcing, a small fire in the inactive records, and countless men learning not to look at her while speaking in front of her.

In the beginning, that hurt. Then it became a tool.

He learned that power relaxes near those who consider it a background. The secretary discusses an implied bribe while she changes the trash bag. The aide arranges the official version while looking at his cell phone as she mops the hallway. The councilman betrays an ally over coffee, believing that the maid cannot tell the name of a construction company from the name of a medicine. Mrs. Odete distinguished everything.

The first gesture of loyalty to Helena came years before the campaign, when she saw her thank for the coffee while looking into her eyes and ask about her hospitalized grandson without the procedural hurry of her superiors. People in power tend to be polite as if discarding. Helena, not. Helena had the uncommon flaw of paying attention.

When the campaign exploded, Odete took three days to decide whether to actually get into the game. She had grown children, retirement in sight, and a horror of others' bravado using the body of a poor woman as a symbolic shield. She watched. She heard the mayor call Helena ungrateful in a closed room, saw Marcos Aurélio ask the cleaning lady to remove a torn document 'discreetly,' and heard the legal team planning to erase email trails as casually as talking about the weather.

On the night she collected from the office basket the first strips of what would later be reconstructed, she carried the bag home in her market bag. On the bus, a young woman complained about the smell of wet paper. Mrs. Odete replied that it was the smell of rich people in a hurry.

At home, she spread the strips out on the kitchen table and stared at the names of contracts, dates, and initials as if staring at a dismembered body. She thought about her grandson studying at a school without decent meals, the street of her sister flooding every summer, the daughter who had lost her job at the health center because the outsourced contract had disappeared overnight. She also thought about her own life, so often reduced to a tray, cloth, and invisibility.

The next day, he handed the bag to Arlete at the print shop.

— If it goes wrong, I'll say it was an old woman's curiosity — warned you.

Arlete held her hand.

— If it works out, you'll have to get used to existing in the plural.

Mrs. Odete didn't understand at the time. She would only understand months later, when she started being stopped on the street by lunch ladies and cleaning assistants who would say 'we saw what you did' in a tone that mixed gratitude and summons.

She never wanted a medal.

I just wanted to stop being confused with the background.

Chapter 20: The Voice of the Streets

The Praça da Matriz was packed before the scheduled time. This time it wasn't a launch, nor curiosity, nor a new effect. It was accumulation. The city was beginning to understand that Helena's campaign was not just about a wounded woman. It was about an entire system finally having to answer to those who had always supported it.

The flatbed truck parked in front of the City Hall. Caio took care of the broadcast. Arlete organized the supporting speeches. Lucas handed out summarized copies of the documents to the local press. Simone, pale, watched from the side with a Fazenda badge hidden in her pants pocket, as if she still needed to prove to herself that she hadn't dreamed the entire journey.

When Helena went up, she took with her a transparent envelope full of reconstructed strips and a USB drive held between her fingers.

— They thought they could crush what they did — he said, pointing to the building behind him. — They thought it was enough to call the people of this city 'low clergy' and continue treating the lunch lady, driver, receptionist, and technician as furniture. But it was that furniture that gathered the proof of the theft in the trash.

She lifted the envelope. The wind moved the strips as if they were streamers from a hard carnival.

— This asphalt was paid for and never arrived. This erosion was known and hidden. This money left the place where you needed it and ended up where it always goes when no one oversees power: in the pockets of the same people and in the propaganda of the same people.

There was no more murmuring in the square. There was the silence of a church and a hospital line. The silence of people understanding.

In the end, when he named Vila Esperança, nurseries, school meals, drainage, and the women who performed miracles with little, the chorus started from the back and began to grow:

— Helena! Helena! Helena!

She did not smile immediately.

Did you know that shouting in the square doesn't win an election by itself.

But he also knew how to recognize when a city, for the first time, began to speak back.

Chapter 21: The Decisive Debate

On the eve of the local television debate, the team rehearsed in an improvised printing studio. Caio played the role of the venomous moderator. Lucas simulated ideological attacks. Arlete, on purpose, portrayed the suspicious voter who wanted to know what would prevent Helena from becoming more of the same. Simone insisted on numbers. Berenice corrected any sentence that sounded too pretty and not concrete enough.

When she actually arrived at the studio, Helena was tired to the bone, but strangely calm. Alberto opened the confrontation section with his well-known paternal tone.

— Helena is a hardworking technician," he said, looking at her with manufactured affection. "But governing is not managing resentment or viral video.

She waited for the mediator to release her answer.

— It's true. Governing is also not outsourcing report reading, ignoring geological risk, and pretending to be surprised when the truth appears on an open network.

The audience reacted with a short noise.

Alberto insisted on the argument of experience, of image, of 'weight.' Marcos Aurélio tried to call her ungrateful. Helena replied with dates, numbers, contracts, and a phrase that Caio had asked her to keep for the exact moment:

— You sign the pen. I signed the work that made the pen worthwhile.

In the final block, he didn't speak to Alberto or Marcos. He spoke to the camera.

— If you are listening to me from your kitchen, from your workshop, from your building's front desk, from the hospital shift, know this: I am not here because I am different from you. I am here because for years I was exactly the kind of person who makes everything work and sees someone else take the credit. They call that support. I call it exploitation.

When the program ended, Caio was in the parking lot with his eyes shining.

— You just turned a spreadsheet into civic revenge — he said.

Helena got into the car without responding.

It wasn't revenge.

It was a return.

Chapter 22: The Fall of the Empire

Alberto's office spent the last week of the campaign in a feverish state. Businessmen who had until then called directly began to 'ask for more time.' A newspaper that had always softened problems published an editorial demanding clarifications. The Public Prosecutor's Office conducted a search at the Department of Public Works based on the reconstructed documents. Marcos Aurélio was sweating so much that he looked sick.

On the inside, the empire did not fall with a scream. It fell with evasion.

Secretary who didn't answer. Ally who sent a hug instead of support. Businessman who postponed dinner. Driver who asked to resign. Power, Helena realized, is only solid as long as it can sell itself as inevitable. When it stops seeming inevitable, it becomes a tired man on the phone.

On the afternoon she learned about the operation at Obras, Helena was sitting on a park bench eating a cold pastry between one appointment and another. Caio received the message first.

— They entered — he said, showing the screen.

She read the short text, looked ahead, and felt something close to mourning. Not for Alberto. For all the time she had believed that structures of that size were natural, almost geological.

— What was it? — asked Caio.

— Nothing. I'm just thinking about how many times I called normal what was just well-decorated abuse.

That same late afternoon, Mrs. Odete sent another message:

Today the water from the tap got hot. Not even the filter wants to work anymore.

Interlude VIII: Alberto Without an Audience

In the final week of the campaign, Alberto discovered what is most humiliating in politics: not public defeat, but the moment when people begin to treat him as if the defeat were already a done deal.

The phone was still ringing, but less. The businessmen still answered, but slowly. The newspaper that for years had softened any scandal now asked for an "official version in writing." Marcos Aurélio prowled the office like a dog that senses a change in the owner of the house.

On the night the Public Prosecutor's search made national news, Alberto asked everyone to leave early. He wanted to think alone. For the first time in decades, the silence of the office did not sound majestic. It sounded like a waiting room.

He poured himself a whiskey that no longer went down well, opened the old folder with campaign speeches, and reread slogans from his first terms. 'City for everyone.' 'Work with seriousness.' 'Human management.' All public men end up keeping some material that proves how much they were once capable of believing in their own performance.

When the maid came in to collect the cups from the late afternoon, he raised his eyes with automatic irritation.

— Later.

Mrs. Odete set the tray on the table.

— Afterwards, you might not be here anymore.

It took him a second to understand what he had heard.

— What?

— Nothing, doctor. I'm just thinking out loud.

She left before he reacted. It was the first time Alberto fully realized what Helena had seen long before: power does not stand just because it commands. It stands because a crowd of people accepts continuing to sustain the farce in silence.

Later, he called an old businessman, one of those who have been on a first-name basis for twenty years.

— We need to talk.

On the other side, the response came back too meek.

— We'll see after the election, Alberto.

It wasn't negative. It was worse.

It was abandonment in polite language.

When he turned it off, he saw his own reflection in the dark window of the office. His face looked the same as always. What had changed was the frame. Without an audience, even the man most trained to seem inevitable discovers that the mirror does not vote.

Chapter 23: The Night of the Pots and Pans

On the night before the election, the printing house was silent like a hospital during closed visiting hours. The maps had already been distributed, the poll workers' schedule reviewed, the cars organized, the messaging groups full of practical guidelines. There wasn't much left to do without getting in the way.

Helena sat on a cardboard box with a mug of tea forgotten in her hands. Caio approached and sat next to her.

— You look like someone who wants to escape a party thrown in your honor — she said.

— I am afraid of winning.

He did not treat the sentence as an exaggeration.

— Me too.

Helena looked at her own hands.

— What if I become everything I criticized? What if I get there and the machine chews me up the same way? What if I disappoint exactly those who helped me get here?

Arlete, on the other side of the table, answered without lifting her eyes from the ballot chart.

— Fear is what differentiates a person in competition from a job holder. Don't try to get rid of it. Just don't let it drive.

Just before ten, the sound started in the distance. First a lone pot, then another, then many. Soon the whole city seemed to be banging metal on metal from the kitchens, the windows, the backyards. They were not fireworks, nor a paid parade. They were women and neighbors turning routine into an instrument.

Helena went to the door of the print shop and stood listening.

In that noise there was childhood, anger, dishes, lack of time, rushed dinner, real life.

— Is this because of the lasagna? — he asked, half laughing, half crying.

Caio rested his shoulder on hers.

— No. This is because of everything that came along.

Chapter 24: The Dawn of Broken Silence

The counting started with the usual cruel pedagogy: the rich neighborhoods first. Alberto took the lead, and the local commentators assumed the tone of inevitability too early. At the print shop, no one spoke loudly. Just the noise of the television, incoming messages, and Caio's fingernails tapping on the table.

— There's still the outskirts — Lucas repeated, like a mantra and calculation.

As the ballot boxes from Vila Esperança, Alto do Cruzeiro, the Industrial neighborhood, and the more distant housing complexes were counted, the distance shrank. Simone cried silently without realizing it. Arlete prayed without admitting that she was praying. Helena kept her body still because she felt that if she moved too much, something inside her would break before its time.

At 11:12 PM, the blue bar surpassed the red one.

No one shouted in the first second. The astonishment came before the joy. Then came the anchor's official announcement, the mathematical confirmation, her name crossing the television as if it belonged to someone else.

Caio was the first to hug her. Lucas came right after. Arlete took a little longer, as if she needed to grant the story a second of respect before touching it.

Outside, the pots returned.

On Helena's cellphone, one last message from Ricardo appeared:

I hope this was worth it.

She read it. She didn't reply. She deleted the contact.

Not all silence is submission.

Sometimes it's just the cleanest way to close a language that no longer serves.

Interlude IX: The Day After the Victory

The day after the election was less epic than the previous dawn and, for that very reason, more truthful.

The print shop woke up smelling of reheated coffee, damp paper, old sweat, and adhesive tape. There were cups on the floor, coats forgotten on chairs, crooked posters on the walls, and four people sleeping in positions that, under any civilized circumstance, would seem impossible. The television was still on mute, repeating the word 'historic' with the obsession of someone who wants to turn surprise into a product.

Helena woke up with a sore neck and a strange feeling of being behind. For a second she thought she needed to review a spreadsheet for Alberto. For another, that she had to remind Ricardo about the car insurance. Then her whole body understood, all at once, where it was in time.

Caio continued sleeping sitting up, chin dropped on his chest, cellphone in hand. Lucas was snoring on the floor with a sweatshirt covering his eyes. Arlete was already awake, smoking outside the door with the posture of someone who does not allow the body the luxury of celebrating before assessing the damage.

— Did you sleep? — asked Helena, going out to the sidewalk.

— A little. You?

— Enough to dream that I lost and that I had to campaign again.

Arlete slowly exhaled smoke.

— You will still dream of worse things. A dream is the body's way of demanding what the mind pretends to manage.

Inside the printing shop, Helena's phone wouldn't stop. Messages from a city councilor, a deputy, people who had ignored her for months and now said 'I've always believed.' There were also the messages that mattered: Maria das Dores sending a photo of breakfast with the caption 'we won'; Dona Nair holding the pot like a trophy; Simone writing only 'thank you for not backing down'; Celina saying 'I already told the family group that whoever calls you ungrateful will hear from me.'

Around ten, a TV crew showed up without warning wanting to capture the atmosphere of the victory. Caio woke up and was consumed with professional anger.

— Yesterday, when we needed a camera in the mud, they sent an intern. Today they show up to film poetic dark circles.

Helena laughed for the first time that morning.

Before noon, the print shop received an improvised line of people wanting to hug, ask for a job, hand over an envelope, suggest a name, offer a service, demand a promise, take a photo. Democracy arrived mixed, unfiltered, as always.

In the midst of the turmoil, Helena noticed something that almost went unnoticed: the campaign was over. The world that had sustained her until then, with its clear urgency and its sharp enemy, was dying in real time. In its place, something else was being born, more ambiguous, longer, harder to love.

Victory was not rest.

It was a maze exchange.

Chapter 25: The Weight of the Sash

On the day of the inauguration, Helena entered the office alone before dawn. The key turned in the lock with a dry click, and the sound echoed down the empty hallway as if opening an old safe. There were no journalists, staff, photographer, or flower on the desk. Just dust, wood, the warm smell of the old carpet, and a silence that, for the first time, did not belong to Alberto.

She closed the door behind her and stood still for a few seconds, both hands still resting on her bag. She had spent years entering there with her body already inclined toward the task: delivering reports, explaining crises, correcting data, waiting for orders. Now she could walk across the room without asking for permission, and this new freedom bore no resemblance to the fantasy that had sustained her on campaign days. She did not feel the kind of triumph that movies like. She felt raw responsibility.

She went to the window. The city outside seemed less grandiose and clearer than before. From up there, she didn't see a model. She saw drainage failures, patched roofs, eroded land, late buses, the imperfect curve of the avenue where the asphalt sank near the municipal market. The office turned everything into a panorama. Helena could no longer see without detailing the damage.

She sat in the main chair and took a few seconds to lean back. The armchair was too wide, made for men who confused ergonomics with a display of power. She adjusted herself uncomfortably, like someone occupying a piece of furniture inherited from a relative who thought the opposite way.

In the side drawer, she found a folder with her name written in pencil: 'Helena - notes and adjustments.' Inside, her projects, her texts, her spreadsheets, her speeches, all with handwritten notes from Alberto: 'use without citing,' 'run by Marcos,' 'keep authorship,' 'good idea, don’t empower,' 'great wording, simplify to make it his.'

Helena read slowly. Not out of difficulty. Out of physical resistance. Each note was a receipt of what she had always known without being able to fully prove: Alberto knew exactly what he was doing. He didn't erase her out of habit. He erased her by method.

She closed the folder and laid her hand on the cover.

Did not cry out of anger.

Cried over lost time.

When the phone vibrated, it was already past six. It was Caio.

The square is filling up. There are people with pots. I don't know if this is a new political culture or a gastronomic threat.

Helena wiped her face with the back of her hand and replied:

Going up ten.

Before leaving, however, he opened the door of the small support room adjoining the office, where for years the anteroom for waiting orders had operated. On the corner table, there was a pile of folders left by the previous staff, an empty pitcher, and a crooked oval mirror. In the mirror, he saw his own face still too young for the logic of men who measure authority by wrinkles. He thought about the prologue of his own life, in that bathroom of the Tourism Department, trying to age artificially with dark lipstick. He smiled without humor.

She no longer needed to look older.

I needed to endure being seen.

Chapter 26: The Empty Office

Electoral victory didn't teach anyone how to govern the next day. The first month was a sequence of too many meetings, too many people, too many expectations. Caio, now government secretary, was no longer just the funny friend; he had to arbitrate councilor conflicts, calm party egos, filter disasters, and learn, in record time, to replace sarcasm with institutional phrasing without dying inside. Simone took on the financial transition and discovered a bigger hole than she had feared. Arlete declined a position but appeared almost every day to remind everyone that winning an election didn't absolve anyone.

Helena, in turn, discovered a new kind of loneliness: the loneliness of someone who stops being invisible, but has not yet gotten used to existing in front of so much expectation.

At the first meeting with the transition team, the chief accountant of the previous administration spoke for twenty minutes about 'inherited constraints,' 'situational challenges,' and 'very harsh fiscal realities.' Simone listened to everything in silence, requesting specific documents with the calm of a person who knows where the other is lying but prefers to choose the best moment to expose the body of the contradiction.

Later, already in her room, she closed the door and handed Helena a printed spreadsheet.

— They left committed contracts without sufficient backing, disguised payables, and two expense sheets for the waterfront that shouldn't exist in the same quarter. If I'm right, it's not just a hole. It's a trap.

— Trap for what?

— For you to take over appearing incompetent in three months.

Helena sat in front of the table and read the first page.

— How long to open all of this?

— With a team I trust? Thirty days. With the team they left? Never.

There was no heroism in running a city hall. There was administrative mud, tight deadlines, pressured employees, suppliers pushing, allies wanting recognition before completing work, and the opposition calling prudence weakness. In the second week, a councilman from the base was already pressuring the Public Works Department for a cousin 'experienced in earthmoving.' In the third week, a businessman allied with the party who had provided cars during the campaign showed up offering 'help' with the new waste collection bid. In the fourth week, a radio host began calling her the 'internet mayor.'

At night, when the building emptied, Helena stayed there, surrounded by decrees, drafts, and a city that could now address her by name. Sometimes she heard the cleaning cart passing in the hallway and felt like going out just to talk to someone who still didn't speak to her so carefully. Other times she would open the news app and find the first wave of criticisms: too young, too media-oriented, too radical, too technical, too political, not political enough.

On a particularly long Friday, after a whole day discussing appointments, Helena stayed staring out of the dark office window and missed something she didn't expect to miss: finished tasks. Dishes, for example. Laundry to put away. A list that ends. The domestic world that had suffocated her had, at least, a beginning, middle, and end. Governing was the opposite of that. It was a job that fed on its own incompleteness.

Just before leaving, he heard a timid knock at the door. It was the schedule secretary, Márcia, a woman in her early fifties, wearing thin glasses and a voice always a few decibels below what it deserved.

— Mayor, I'm sorry to interrupt. I just wanted to let you know that the maid has left fresh coffee. You haven't had lunch.

Helena almost automatically answered that everything was fine, that he could leave it, that there was no need to worry. Instead, she asked:

— Márcia, what time did you arrive today?

The woman seemed surprised.

— Seven fifteen.

— And what time will it leave?

— When you finish signing next week's agendas.

Helena looked at the pile of papers, at her careful expression, at the ease with which that system demanded that another woman stretch her own day to sustain her role. She felt the old gear offer itself as a reflection: accept, thank, move on. She did not accept.

— Sit down for a minute — he said. — Let's reorganize this.

Márcia hesitated before pulling out the chair. And it was in that small embarrassment that Helena saw, with painful clarity, how power seeps into speech before it seeps into the pen.

Chapter 27: The City When It Rains

The first major crisis came before the hundred days.

In April, a six-hour storm flooded entire sections of the outskirts and turned Vila Esperança into a map of mud, floating furniture, and low-hanging wires. The same drains that Helena had denounced during her campaign did not hold up. The opposition was quick: 'speech doesn't drain the street,' said a councilman in a video recorded from the dry balcony of his apartment. Alberto, now smaller, reappeared as a resentful commentator on a local radio station, pretending institutional concern over 'the lack of preparation of the new administration.'

Helena received the first phone call at four twenty-three in the morning. The second came while she was still putting on her boot. By five, she was already in the official car with a map open on her lap, listening to fragmented reports from Civil Defense, social assistance, the public works department, and residents who called her number directly because during the campaign she had the terrible and good idea of sharing it too much.

At the entrance of Vila Esperança, the water had already receded enough to reveal the trail: a waterlogged mattress leaning against a post, bags caught on the wire, a children's toy floating in a thick puddle, a dog shivering on a low roof. Dona Nair appeared in flip-flops and a dress wet up to her thigh.

— I knew you were coming — I said, before any greeting. — I just hope you’re not coming to take a photo and leave dry.

— If I wanted that, I would have sent an assistant — replied Helena.

He spent the next thirty-six hours almost without sitting. He went from house to house, from school to shelter, from emergency meeting to hostile interview. Simone managed the finances to authorize emergency purchases without repeating the cycle of shady contracts. Caio took hits on social media and in the press until he managed to turn accounting into understandable information. Lucas coordinated volunteers in an improvised reception center and confronted a pastor, a police officer, and a merchant at the same time when they tried to block trans teenagers from a collective shelter space. Arlete showed up unannounced and started distributing tasks as if she had never left the front line.

In the afternoon, a reporter surrounded her in the middle of the mud.

— Mayor, do you acknowledge that you failed?

Helena looked at the camera, with mud on the hem of her pants and hair stuck to her face.

— I acknowledge that this city has failed this neighborhood for decades. And that now it no longer has the right to feign surprise.

The speech spread too quickly, and for hours she was called both brave and opportunistic. Helena didn't have time to manage the aftermath. At midnight, covered in dirty water up to her ankles, she went into a school bathroom and faced her own reflection in the small mirror. She looked older than she had during the entire campaign. Not because of lines on her face. Because of the exhaustion of finally being accountable for something she had previously only denounced.

At dawn, she slept for twenty-seven minutes in a plastic chair in the coordination office and woke up with the phone vibrating in her hand. There were four more streets closed. There was criticism on the radio. There was a secretary demanding a signature. There was a mother wanting medicine for her asthmatic daughter. The entire city was reaching her body without mediation.

And yet, there, with the clay drying on my skin and my head throbbing, I knew that that was the only possible place.

Chapter 28: The Price of the Pen

Governing required from Helena a learning that no campaign teaches: how not to become the new exploiter of the people who brought her there.

She noticed this the first time she almost asked Simone for a report due "yesterday" without asking how many hours the other woman had already been awake. She noticed it when she saw Caio sleeping on the office sofa after three consecutive early mornings, still with his laptop open on his chest. She noticed it when Márcia apologized for not being able to foresee an impossible-to-anticipate conflict and Helena heard, in that apology, the old voice of herself.

The definitive snap came on a Wednesday, almost midnight, when he entered the smaller communication room to pick up a draft and found the youngest press officer, Juliana, silently crying while correcting a press release.

— What happened? — asked Helena.

The girl jumped up, wiping her face in embarrassment.

— Nothing, mayor. Sorry. I'm just finishing up.

— Juliana.

She took a long time to respond.

— My mother has been hospitalized since yesterday. I leave here and go to the hospital. But I thought I needed to finish this first because you asked for it today.

Helena felt the blood drain away for a second.

He had not asked out of cruelty. He had asked because everything seemed urgent to him. And there lay the most dangerous trap of power: it allows you to hurt without raising your voice.

The following week, she canceled two meetings, reorganized workflows, implemented a time limit for the team, and opened a fixed listening space for lower-level employees. She created a simple rule that upset many people in the party: urgency would have to come with justification, not with managerial habit. She was criticized for 'lack of toughness.' One councilman even said, laughing, that the city hall was not a therapeutic center. Arlete called her naive in public. Later, in private, she admitted:

— Maybe this is the first time I see someone trying to reach power without immediately reproducing its logic.

Even so, the price of the pen charged.

Every small concession made to approve a project left a bad taste. Every rejected name generated a new enmity. Every public decision tore from her a piece of the old fantasy that the right side of history would always be clear. By refusing to appoint an engineer connected to an allied businessman to the Department of Public Works, she lost three votes in the Chamber and gained a week of smiling blackmail. By demanding transparency in a collection contract, she heard from the party president that 'excessive purity is also vanity'.

One night, leaving the office late, Helena stopped at a newsstand as it was closing and bought a packet of saltine crackers. The owner recognized her, offered a discount, and called her mayor with a mix of respect and familiarity. She thanked him, paid in full, and continued walking to the official car, thinking about how much she missed being anonymous enough to buy anything without becoming a symbol.

In the car, he opened the package and ate two dry cookies while looking at the city through the window. Then he wrote on his phone, in notes:

Power does not corrupt only through luxury. It corrupts through haste, through centrality, through the idea that your time is worth more.

Chapter 29: One Year in Office

One year of government did not bring consecration. It brought maturity in accountability.

The toughest episode of that period came when Lucas, already leading the youth and diversity coordination, launched a week of actions against school violence and decided to include a public circle with LGBTQIA+ students. The program was simple: listening, legal guidance, support, and training for educators. That was enough for the reaction to come loaded with moral panic.

On the morning of the event, a group of protesters appeared at Library Square with signs about "family," "ideology," and "protection of children." There were religious leaders, two councilmen seeking the camera, and a group of parents genuinely scared because they had been fed cheap lies for years. Lucas arrived early, saw the movement, and turned pale.

— "If you want to cancel, I understand," said Helena, who was already by his side before the press set up the first tripod.

Lucas took a deep breath.

— If I cancel, they always learn which button to press.

She nodded.

In the impromptu meeting with journalists, they asked if the city hall was 'indoctrinating minors.' Helena replied without raising her voice:

— The city hall is doing what it failed to do for a long time: preventing children and adolescents from being crushed by silence. If someone calls this a threat to the family, they may need to reconsider what they understand by family.

The speech earned a hostile headline, a twisted meme, emotional support, an anonymous threat, and a teacher's praise. Above all, it produced a scene that Helena did not forget: at the end of the event, a mother approached with her thirteen-year-old daughter by the hand. The girl had slumped shoulders and hair cut in a crooked line, as if someone had done it at home in a hurry.

— I came to fight — said the woman, without embellishment. — Then I heard the boy talking over there — she pointed to Lucas — and I realized that my daughter has had his little face for a year. I think I got here late, but I got here.

Lucas cried in the car afterward. Not in front of the team, but next to Helena, in the parking lot.

— Sometimes I forget that work is not about winning an argument, he said. It is about opening a door for someone to walk through.

Helena thought about her own campaign, about the lasagna, about the first time she had named the prison.

— And sometimes the door takes years to be seen — he replied.

Chapter 30: The Council of Kitchens

The council was born almost by accident, after a meeting in which four lunch ladies, two daycare cooks, and Dona Nair spent over an hour correcting, with brutal patience, the technocratic good intentions of the city hall. Helena left there with the feeling of having received an entire lesson without being entitled to a diploma.

The Council of Kitchens was then formalized: a permanent listening space with lunch ladies, caregivers, community cooks, women from neighborhood associations, cleaning staff, and mothers of students. Many people thought the name was ridiculous. Some secretaries suggested something more 'institutional.' Helena did not give in.

At the first official meeting, an undersecretary of Education presented flawless slides about the improvement of school meals. When he finished, Dona Odete asked for the floor.

— The slide is beautiful — he said, adjusting his glasses. — But at the CMEI in Alto do Cruzeiro, the beans came two times with stones. So I wanted to know if in the chart the child also chews PowerPoint.

No one laughed out loud, but no one forgot.

From that point on, the council became one of the spaces that produced the most discomfort in the government. Not because of performative radicalism, but because it worked with a resource little tolerated by those in power: concrete experience. The women would arrive with notebooks, receipts, photos, precise memories of dates. They knew exactly where public policy turned into a lack.

Helena started leaving those meetings more tired and more protected at the same time. Tired because governing exposed to reality is real work. Protected because the council prevented her from falling in love more with the beautiful solution than with the real problem.

One of those afternoons, at the end of the meeting, Dona Nair was the last to leave.

— I'll tell you something that your people won't say because they're afraid of seeming ungrateful — he said. — You still explain too much when you're insecure.

Helena laughed, hit full on.

— And how do I do it?

— Talk less and deliver more. People already understand that you think. Now they want to know if the water will flow down the right street.

Helena wrote down the sentence on her cellphone right in front of her.

It was perhaps the most honest compliment I had ever received in my life.

Interlude VI: The Office at Two Thirteen

There were nights when the office became an almost foreign place. After the last light in the hallway went out and the cleaning footsteps disappeared down the stairs, there remained the low hum of the air conditioner, the reflection of the city in the window, and the feeling that all power, seen in the early morning, seems less like glory than insomnia.

At two thirteen on a Thursday without an exceptional crisis, Helena was still sitting at the table signing an agreement for the renovation of a health post. Her body was asking for bed. Her mind was asking for one more reading. Somewhere down the corridor, someone knocked over a chair. The sound made her lift her eyes as if she were still at the Tourism Department, ready to put out someone else's fire.

There was no one. Only her.

She went to the office bathroom and looked at her own face in the mirror above the sink. There was no longer the youthful desperation to look older. There was, however, another conflict: the difficulty of existing outside work even after having earned the right to choose whom she worked for.

He grabbed the cell phone on impulse. He almost sent a message to Caio. He almost opened the team group. He almost replied to an email. Instead, he called his mother.

Celina answered sleepily on the third ring.

— Did something happen? — he asked, his voice still caught in sleep.

Helena thought about saying no. But something diffuse and important had happened: she was sad in a way without a headline.

— No. I just didn't want to hear my own head alone.

On the other side, the mother remained silent for a moment.

— Did you have dinner?

Helena laughed, defeated.

— No.

— So turn it off, go eat something, and then call me tomorrow. No governance works with low glucose.

— You made up the word now.

— And it turned out good.

Helena hung up smiling. She opened the drawer, took out a forgotten packet of nuts, and ate them one by one by the window. For the first time in months, she admitted to herself something simple: she had won, she had changed the city on many fronts, she had refused servitude. And yet, she was still a woman alone in front of a huge building trying to learn the shape of her own life.

This recognition did not diminish her.

On the contrary.

Made victory habitable.

Chapter 31: Simone Closes the Books

When the internal audit of the waterfront was finally finished, Simone took the report printed in two copies, stapled with the obsession of someone who knows that paper can also protect sanity. It was one hundred and forty-three pages of overpricing, expense splitting, invoices without physical correspondence, addenda without technical justification, and a sequence of emails that showed, with less drama than truth, that fraud had been methodical.

She put the battery on Helena's table and stood up.

— If I sit down, I might not get up again today.

Helena pulled the chair toward her.

— Sit down anyway.

Simone sat down. She had deep dark circles under her eyes and an almost feverish calm.

— I spent eleven years looking at this kind of trace as if I were seeing water damage on the wall of a rented house. I knew it was serious, I knew someone was going to pay, but I kept living there because it seemed impossible to take down the whole building.

— And now?

— Now I know that the building doesn't fall by itself. Someone needs to point out where the column is rotten.

The following week, Simone gave a statement to the Public Prosecutor's Office. She left there trembling more than she showed. On the courthouse steps, she ran into a former boss from the Treasury who gave her a thin smile.

— There's still time to say that she was pressured by the mayor.

Simone answered without changing her voice:

— There is still time for you to learn to sign your own end without help.

When he told the scene to the team, Caio clapped alone in the room.

But heroism had a cost. Rumors began to circulate that Simone was ungrateful, unbalanced, resentful. A blog published an old photo of her at a department gathering to insinuate opportunism. Her mother called, asking if it would be better to 'let the dust settle.' Her ex-husband sent a message offering unsolicited advice.

Simone almost gave in the following Sunday, when she spent hours staring at the pile of clothes to wash and thinking that maybe she had gone too far for a city that had never explicitly asked anything of her. In the evening, she received a photo from Dona Odete: lunch ladies gathered around the cellphone, all holding the link to the article about the audit as if they were holding a proof of existence.

Caption: "You are not alone."

It was enough.

Interlude X: Caio Without Irony

There was a period when Caio was afraid of becoming the type of man he had always hated.

The sign appeared on a mundane Tuesday, when a new assistant entered the room with an unpolished text for review and he, exhausted, responded more curtly than he intended. The young woman left in silence, her shoulders tense. Ten minutes later, Caio realized that he no longer remembered the exact last sentence he had said, only its effect.

He spent the rest of the morning restless.

Being a Government secretary did not resemble at all being the "social media kid." Before, his intelligence worked through infiltration, mockery, and improvisation. Now he had to decide the flow, cut demand, mediate a resentful councilor, filter the egos of the secretariat, negotiate with the Chamber without giving up his soul, and still keep the political core breathing. He discovered that formal power has its own drug: the feeling that your impatience is always partially justified.

At night, already after ten, he entered Helena's room without knocking.

— I think I'm becoming unbearable — he/she announced.

She lifted her eyes from the document she was reading.

— Specify.

— I snapped at people who didn't deserve it. I'm starting to treat urgency as a natural right of my role. Yesterday I dreamed about a voting spreadsheet. Today I woke up angry at a councilman even before remembering his name.

Helena closed the folder.

— This has partial cure. But it needs monitoring.

Caio threw himself into the front chair.

— The worst part is that now I understand a lot about the men from the old office. Not the corruption, but the vice of centrality. Everyone goes through the day wanting a piece of you. At some point, you start to think that your fatigue is worth more than that of others.

Helena smiled without joy.

— Welcome to the reason why it is so easy to reproduce what we swore to fight against.

They remained silent for a few seconds.

— And how do you do it? — he asked.

— I don't do it all the time. I try to realize quickly when I start thinking that I am the center of how things work. Then I look for someone who isn't afraid of finding me annoying.

Caio smiled.

— Arlete.

— Arlete.

The next day, he sought out the advisor to whom he had spoken rudely and apologized without any elaborate justification. The girl was so surprised that he almost hated himself for not having found it strange before that an apology was a rare thing in the office.

On the way back to the room, he opened the notebook on his cell phone and wrote:

Do not let the role replace the person. Do not let fatigue become a moral hierarchy.

Then he sent the sentence to Helena.

She replied with a single word:

Fits.

Chapter 32: The Slow Work

Helena discovered that there are works that take long not because of corruption or lack of will, but because the honest path is annoyingly slower than the shortcut. The drainage project in Vila Esperança progressed at an ant's pace compared to the speed of campaign speeches. Contested bidding, administrative appeal, winning company without capacity, correction of the public notice, new publication, technical visit, recommendation from the court of accounts.

On a Monday morning, after hearing for the fourth time that week that the project 'was still on paper,' Helena lost her patience in the meeting.

— So tell me clearly where the bottleneck is," he asked, looking at Works, Legal and Finance. "I don't want abstraction. I want the exact name of the delay.

The chief engineer, a man too cautious to climb in life and too cowardly to get out of it, adjusted his glasses.

— The bottleneck is that you want to do everything right that, historically, was done quickly because it was poorly done.

Helena remained silent for three seconds.

It was the most useful sentence he had heard in months.

That afternoon, he went to Vila Esperança without notifying the press. He found the main street half open, half dry mud, and a circle of residents watching the machine stopped because a signature from the technical inspector was missing for the next section.

— If I signed off half-heartedly, you would have the street today and the collapse three years from now — she said to a man who accused her of slowness.

— And if you sign honestly, how long are we stuck in the mud? — he replied.

Helena didn't get a good answer. Only the true one.

— Even the time that a serious process takes, without stealing from you along the way.

The man grumbled, but he didn't leave.

At the end of the visit, Dona Nair appeared with coffee brewed in an aluminum pot.

— Do you know what is worse than being late? — he asked.

— What?

— We don't know if it's a delay or a scam. With you, at least, it's possible to tell the difference. It doesn't improve the mud, but it improves the anger.

It was a little thing. It was a lot.

Chapter 33: The Woman Who Walks Alone

There was one night, close to completing a year and a half in office, when Helena left the office early enough for the city to still be alive in a usual way. There was no event, interview, inauguration, or immediate crisis. Just any ordinary Tuesday.

She asked the driver to let her off two blocks before her house and walked. She wanted, for a few minutes, to feel her own body in motion without the mediation of function. She passed by a bakery closing for the day, a group of teenagers sitting on the wall of a square, a twenty-four-hour pharmacy, a couple arguing softly at the door of a building. It was strangely touching to realize that the world continued, in part, indifferent to her presence.

At the traffic light on Central Avenue, Ricardo saw him on the other side of the street.

He was thinner, without a ring, carrying a shopping bag and wearing an expression less smooth than before. He also saw her. He hesitated. Then he crossed.

— Helena.

— Ricardo.

There was no immediate anger between the two. Time had sanded the surface of the damage without erasing its structure.

— Are you okay? — he asked, the kind of question that, years before, would have been a trap.

— Sometimes.

He let out a half smile.

— I learned to cook two things.

She almost laughed.

— Which ones?

— Pasta and omelet. None very well.

The following silence was not comfortable, but it did not need to be cruel either.

— I was unfair to you — said Ricardo, looking more at the traffic than at her. — It took me a while to understand that what I called care was actually a fear of losing the easy life.

Helena listened without offering absolution.

— Understanding late doesn't change what you did.

— I know.

A horn blared behind them. The light turned green. The city pushed the conversation back into the flow.

— Good evening, Ricardo.

— Good evening, Helena.

She kept walking. She felt no desire to return, nor to prolong the pain to confirm that she had survived. She felt only a kind of rare stillness. Sometimes closure does not come as repair. It comes as a lack of appetite for the past.

Chapter 34: The City Learns to Demand

Two years after the election, the biggest change was not just in the visible works or in the ongoing judicial processes. It was in the city's behavior towards power.

Public hearings stopped being empty theaters. The school meal council was packed. Neighborhood meetings already came with a printed spreadsheet and written questions. People who used to accept 'we will study it' as an answer began to ask about deadlines, sources of funding, bidding stages, and the name of the responsible technician. Local newspapers, deprived of the previously blind funding, became more aggressive and, curiously, more useful. Radio stations discovered that interviewing a prepared resident yielded more than reproducing statements from the office.

This produced an ambiguous effect on Helena. It was the city she had dreamed of, but she had dreamed perhaps without imagining that living in it would be so exhausting.

At a hearing on mobility, a high school student stood up and dismantled an entire presentation with a fifteen-second question about bus routes and departure times. In another, a cook pointed out a discrepancy between the official photo of a renovation and the actual ceiling leak at the health center. At a participatory budget meeting, three women from the suburbs dismantled the priority of a scenic square downtown with a precision that would embarrass many consultants.

Caio summarized the situation better than any analysis:

— You did it. They learned to respond back. Now hang in there.

Helena endured.

And, in secret, sometimes he thanked for the end of the one-sided comfort.

Chapter 35: The Refuge Once More

At a small fundraising event for a cultural festival resumed without fraud, without makeup, and without a ghost manager involved, Helena returned to the Refuge. The bar had the same lights, the same smell of the night, the same feeling of temporary shelter. She sat at the counter where Caio had handed her the black notebook and stayed silent for a few seconds, listening to the sound of the place.

Caio appeared on the other side with two glasses of sparkling water.

— We have evolved little — he/she commented.

— In some ways, a lot.

— In celebration hydration, clearly not.

Helena laughed. There were new marks on Caio's face, small lines that did not diminish him. They did the opposite: they gave shape to the whole person he had always been.

— Did you notice," he said, resting his elbows on the counter, "that this place is no longer a hiding place and has become an official memory?

— Don't talk like that or the historical heritage will appear.

They stayed for a few seconds just listening to the music. Then, as if in a delayed response to something said years before, Caio asked:

— So? Did you find out the difference?

Helena knew what he was talking about.

— Between being necessary and belonging?

He nodded.

— I discovered that belonging is not being indispensable. It's being able to exist fully without needing to prove yourself useful all the time.

Caio raised the glass in a silent toast.

Chapter 36: What Changed in the City

At the end of the event, a sixteen-year-old girl approached with a school notebook under her arm. Her hair was hastily tied back, worn-out sneakers on her feet, and that kind of fragile courage that first shows up in the jaw.

— Mayor, can I ask a question?

— You can.

— Before I thought that whoever ruled the city had to look like a big man. Now I keep thinking if it's possible to rule with the face of someone who still has a lot to hear.

Helena smiled.

— You can. You just can't send it alone.

The girl nodded as if storing the sentence in a useful place.

Later, already in the car, Helena kept thinking about the answer. Not because she was proud of it, but because she recognized in that teenager's body the same kind of old attention that had once taught her to disappear to fit in. Perhaps what had really changed in the city was not just the construction, the procedures, the councils, the portals, and the inspections.

Perhaps the most important thing was this:

a girl could now imagine power without having to imagine it with a man's face.

Epilogue: The Woman Who Stayed

That night, already at home, Helena opened the desk drawer and found the black notebook next to another, new, with a yellow cover. The black one contained the memory of what needed to be broken. The yellow one, the list of what still needed to be built. Neither of them fit anymore in the old idea of usefulness.

He sat in front of the table for a few minutes without opening any. There was a good and bad kind of tiredness spread across his shoulders at the same time. The good came from the work that, despite everything, had left concrete marks on the world. The bad came from the awareness that no city is ever finished and that every public achievement carries the seed of infinite maintenance.

Helena went to the kitchen, filled a glass of water, and stood against the counter in silence for a while. There was no husband waiting for dinner. There was no mayor waiting for a report to sign as if it were his. There was no longer the old impulse to go around collecting other people's chaos before asking if they really wanted to do it.

I still liked arranging things. I was still good at sustaining processes, organizing workflows, anticipating problems. The difference is that now, choosing whom to serve was part of my own dignity. I no longer spontaneously accepted that skill equaled obligation. I no longer confused love with constant demand, nor recognition with well-groomed exploitation.

She opened the refrigerator, found a pot of soup made over the weekend, and smiled at the banality of the gesture. She was increasingly fond of small routines that did not demand political meaning. Heating food. Watering a plant. Taking off her shoes without being watched. Reading fifteen pages without needing to extract a management idea from them. There was a humble freedom in these scenes, and perhaps that was precisely why she cherished them so much.

Outside, the city was still making noise. Bus, motorcycle, dog, distant television, everyday life. Helena opened the window.

For many years, she believed she would be seen on the day she was useful enough.

It took a long time to understand that no visibility is worth the price of disappearing from oneself.

She looked at the street for a few more seconds. She thought of the girl from the school notebook. Of Simone in front of the audit pile. Of Caio, who had taught her to differentiate irony from cowardice. Of Arlete, who had never let her love the symbol more than the world it was meant to serve. Of Mrs. Odete, Mrs. Nair, Lucas, Márcia, Juliana, Celina. Of all the women who, in one way or another, had helped shift the axis of the possible.

Then he closed the window, turned off the kitchen light, and went to sleep without leaving a note, apology, or list of tasks for the next morning.

The world would continue existing without her for a few hours.

And that, finally, no longer seemed like a threat.
